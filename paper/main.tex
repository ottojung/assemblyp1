\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Maximum likelihood offers a natural way to infer a genome from sequencing
data: choose the genome under which the observed reads are most probable.
But is the genome that generated the reads necessarily the most likely one?
We investigate this question in two settings: when only a finite sample
of reads is available, and when the underlying distribution of reads is
known exactly.

For finite samples, we examine a question raised by Shomorony et al.\ (2016):
do the conditions that guarantee genome reconstruction also guarantee that
maximum likelihood selects the true genome? We establish conditions under
which the answer is yes, but also construct counterexamples in which the reads
satisfy the original reconstruction conditions and yet an incorrect genome
has greater likelihood. Thus, the information needed to reconstruct a genome
does not always make it the most likely explanation of the data.

When the read distribution is known exactly, the true genome always maximizes
expected log-likelihood. We establish conditions on genomic repeats under which
this distribution uniquely identifies the true genome. We further show that
these conditions yield a quantitative guarantee for finite samples: the
probability of maximum-likelihood error decreases exponentially as the number
of reads increases.
\end{abstract}
