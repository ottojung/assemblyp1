\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genome assembly aims to reconstruct a genome from the short DNA sequences
produced by sequencing. Under suitable conditions, the overlaps between these
sequences allow us to recover the original genome uniquely. But does this also
guarantee that the true genome is the one most likely to produce the particular
collection of sequences we observed? We investigate when this is the case
and when it fails.

We exhibit finite collections of reads that satisfy strong coverage and
repeat-bridging conditions but are more probable under an incorrect genome.
We also prove a positive result: for oriented reads and a restricted class of
same-length genomes with the required overlap support, the true genome is
the unique maximum-likelihood reconstruction, up to rotation.

We then consider an idealized experiment in which the probabilities of all
possible reads are known exactly. We prove that these probabilities identify the truth uniquely up to rotation
among primitive genomes satisfying the same explicit repeat restriction.
For this class, we also obtain an explicit upper bound on the probability
of a maximum-likelihood error that decreases exponentially with the number
of sampled reads.

\end{abstract}
