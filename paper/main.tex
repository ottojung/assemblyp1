\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genome assembly seeks to reconstruct a DNA sequence from many short reads. A
natural approach is to choose the genome that makes the observed reads most
likely. But under what conditions is this choice guaranteed to be correct?

We establish both positive and negative results. First, we prove a
combinatorial rigidity theorem showing that restrictions on repeated DNA
sequences can uniquely determine the frequencies of genomic fragments
consistent with an assembly graph. Under suitable conditions, this yields
guarantees for maximum-likelihood reconstruction, including uniqueness of the
recovered genome up to circular rotation. We then demonstrate the limits of
these guarantees through explicit counterexamples in which an incorrect genome
has greater likelihood. These examples reveal how genome length, the
representation of DNA strands, and the random sampling of reads affect
reconstruction.

Finally, we study maximum likelihood when the read distribution is known
exactly, without sampling fluctuations. In this setting, the true genome always
maximizes likelihood, but uniqueness remains a nontrivial mathematical
question. We prove that uniqueness holds for a class of primitive genomes
satisfying a structural condition on repeats.

Together, these results clarify when likelihood-based genome assembly succeeds,
why it can fail, and which mathematical properties of DNA sequences determine
the outcome.
\end{abstract}
