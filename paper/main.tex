\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genome assembly seeks to reconstruct the genome from which a collection of
short DNA reads originated. Because sequencing involves random sampling, we can
ask how likely we would have been to observe those same reads if they had come
from a different genome. Is the true genome necessarily the one that makes the
observed reads most likely?

We establish both positive and negative results. First, we prove a
combinatorial rigidity theorem showing that restrictions on repeated DNA
sequences can uniquely determine the frequencies of genomic fragments
consistent with an assembly graph. Under suitable conditions, this implies that
the true genome maximizes likelihood among the relevant same-length candidates,
uniquely up to circular rotation. We then demonstrate the limits of these
guarantees through explicit counterexamples in which an incorrect genome has
greater likelihood. These examples reveal how candidate length, the
representation of DNA strands, and random sampling of reads affect likelihood
rankings.

Finally, we study maximum likelihood when the read distribution is known
exactly, without sampling fluctuations. In this setting, the true genome always
maximizes likelihood, but uniqueness remains a nontrivial mathematical
question. We prove that uniqueness holds for a class of primitive genomes
satisfying a structural condition on repeats.

Together, these results clarify when the true genome has the greatest
likelihood, when another genome can be more likely, and when the maximizer is
unique.
\end{abstract}
