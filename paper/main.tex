\begin{abstract}
\noindent
In genome assembly, we observe short DNA sequences sampled from an unknown genome. A sequencing model tells us the probability of obtaining these observations from any proposed genome. We investigate these probabilities, in particular how they depend on genome structure.

We find that the behavior of maximum likelihood depends strongly on assumptions about the observations and the genomes being considered. Conditions that guarantee reconstruction do not necessarily make the true genome the most likely explanation of a finite sample. Allowing genomes of arbitrary length can lead to overfitting of random fluctuations in read counts, and in some cases the greatest possible likelihood is not attained by any genome. On the other hand, suitable restrictions on genome structure and length can guarantee that the true genome is distinguishable from alternatives, with quantitative bounds on the probability of selecting an incorrect genome.

An important factor is repetition: the same short sequence may occur at several positions in a genome, affecting its likelihood even when the set of possible reads remains unchanged. We study these relationships using read-overlap graphs and their flows, which provide a way to describe possible read frequencies and analyze likelihood optimization.

These findings connect the combinatorial structure of genomes with the statistical properties of their sequencing data, and clarify when likelihood optimization can reliably identify the underlying genome.

\end{abstract}
