\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A genome is a long string that a sequencer reveals only through short,
overlapping reads. Reconstructing it is a combinatorial problem: overlapping
reads form a graph, repeats create competing traversals, and sufficiently
informative reads resolve those ambiguities. The Not-So-Greedy algorithm of
Shomorony et al.\ (2016) turns such information into a unique Eulerian
reconstruction. This paper investigates a related statistical question:
\emph{when is the true genome also the most likely explanation of the
observed reads?} Maximum likelihood is a natural benchmark because, with
the correct sampling model and a uniform prior on a finite candidate class,
it maximizes average reconstruction correctness among all decision rules.
This decision-theoretic guarantee motivates the investigation; it does not
answer the assembly-specific question. We separate a conditional reading, in
which the candidate set is restricted to the feasible fiber, from the
unconditional comparison the 2016 sentence actually poses.

We prove a positive rigidity theorem: under explicit bridging conditions the
true length-$L$ spectrum is the unique positive integer circulation of its
window-support graph. This yields a same-length oriented likelihood result
under the stated candidate hypotheses. We also exhibit exact finite-sample
counterexamples: because observed read counts fluctuate, another genome can
assign them higher probability even when reconstruction is feasible.
Finally, at the population level the true genome always maximizes expected
log-likelihood, and the maximizer is unique up to rotation in the primitive
P2-admissible class. These results connect graph reconstruction,
repeat combinatorics, statistical optimization, and kernel-checked formal proof.
\end{abstract}
