\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A genome is a circular word observed through randomly sampled short reads.
Read overlaps allow reconstruction when repeats are sufficiently bridged,
yet a reconstruction guarantee does not automatically rank the truth highest
under maximum likelihood. We examine this distinction in the setting of
Shomorony et al.'s (2016) question about bridging and maximum likelihood.
Under the project's oriented, same-length, full-support assumptions and
explicit truth candidacy, bridging forces a unique positive circulation
and a unique genome up to rotation. This is a structural rigidity theorem:
every admissible candidate has the truth's spectrum, so likelihood plays
no part in distinguishing candidates on that slice. Dropping either truth candidacy or equal candidate length permits
strict finite-sample likelihood reversals. Same-length molecule counterexamples also satisfy the
historical matched-string bridging predicate.

At the population level the truth always maximizes expected
log-likelihood; within the oriented primitive-P2 class it is
identifiable up to rotation. We further prove that every primitive-P2
genome has length at most $2q^{L-1}$ for alphabet size $q$ and read length
$L$, making the entire class finite. For $n$ independent reads its
wrong-candidate probability is bounded by
$\sum_{D\not\sim S}\rho(p_S,p_D)^n$, the sum of the \emph{$n$-th powers}
of Hellinger affinities. This is an exponential rate, not necessarily
a usable numerical read-depth estimate. Exact per-truth ML success can
oscillate with sample size. The principal structural and statistical
results are kernel-checked in Lean.
\end{abstract}
