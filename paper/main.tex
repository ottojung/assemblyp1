\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genome assembly aims to reconstruct a genome from the short DNA sequences
produced by sequencing. Under suitable conditions, the overlaps between these
sequences allow us to recover the original genome uniquely. But does this also
guarantee that the true genome is the one most likely to produce the particular
collection of sequences we observed? We investigate when this is the case
and when it fails.

In a simple model of error-free, independently sampled reads, a strong
failure occurs when candidate genomes may have arbitrary lengths:
for every fixed genome with branching read overlaps, the probability
that the true genome maximizes exact likelihood tends to zero as
more reads are collected. Allowing unrestricted candidates lets
likelihood fit random variations in the observed frequencies, even
while those frequencies approach their true values. We quantify
this effect and give an exact test for whether a proposed genome is
most likely among genomes of every length.

We also show that a wrong genome can be more likely even when
the observed overlaps satisfy established conditions for reconstructing
the truth. Such failures occur for equal-length competitors as well
when the observations group together reads from opposite DNA strands.

There are positive results too. With suitable restrictions on repeats,
a bound on genome length can distinguish the truth even from competitors
that obey no repeat restriction; stronger shared restrictions give
exponentially decreasing error probabilities as more reads arrive.
We establish the sharp order of one such rate and give a
length-penalized estimator that always has a best candidate and
eventually recovers a suitable fixed truth. The results distinguish
reconstructing a genome, identifying it from its full read
distribution, and finding it reliably from finitely many reads.

\end{abstract}
