\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genome assembly seeks to reconstruct the genome from which a collection of
short DNA reads originated. Because sequencing involves random sampling, every
candidate genome induces a probability distribution over datasets, and the
maximum-likelihood (ML) rule --- score each candidate by the probability it
assigns to the observed reads, output the highest scorer --- is the
conceptually simplest principled statistical benchmark for this task: it has
no tuning parameters, and under a uniform prior over genome equivalence
classes with zero-one loss it maximizes the prior-averaged chance of correct
classification at every sample size. The 2016 question of Shomorony, Kim,
Courtade, and Tse asks whether their repeat-bridging conditions, which suffice
for exact structural reconstruction, also make the true genome the ML genome.

We show that this question splits into two. If the candidate set is
constrained to the genomes that are themselves feasible for the observation,
then --- knowing the truth is feasible --- the conditional problem has a
unique answer, the truth; this is a corollary of the reconstruction theorem,
not a new ML result. If instead the original likelihood is maximized over
its original candidate universe, assuming only that the truth is feasible,
the implication fails: we exhibit finite observations in which an incorrect
genome is strictly more likely, under the exact multinomial, under the
fixed-length binomial approximation, and under the source's own read-string
bridging predicate. These failures are pointwise finite-sample events, not
paradoxes: they coexist with prior-average Bayes optimality and with
consistency, and they leave the positive results intact --- same-length
circular rigidity with uniqueness up to rotation, and, at the population
level, truth-maximality for every candidate class plus uniqueness for
primitive P2 genomes, both kernel-checked with no external premise.

Together, these results clarify when the true genome has the greatest
likelihood, when another genome can be more likely, and why the answer turns on
a truth-dependent conditioning choice the published sentence does not make.
\end{abstract}
