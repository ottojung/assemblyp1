\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genome assembly reconstructs a DNA sequence from short, overlapping reads.
The overlaps constrain how fragments fit together, while maximum likelihood
ranks candidate genomes by how well they explain the observed frequencies of
read sequences. When do these two forms of evidence lead to the same
reconstruction? We investigate this question for error-free reads from circular
genomes, separating the effects of repeat structure, candidate restrictions,
and finite sampling.

Our central positive result is a combinatorial rigidity theorem: excluding
sufficiently long triple repeats forces the true length-$L$ read spectrum to be
the unique positive circulation of its overlap-support graph with the correct
total length. Together with the appropriate bridging hypothesis and an explicit
truth-admissibility condition, this gives maximum-likelihood recovery and
uniqueness up to rotation among same-length oriented candidates. But these
guarantees do not extend indiscriminately. We give kernel-checked finite
counterexamples under exact multinomial and fixed-size binomial likelihoods,
including same-length alternatives, and under reverse-complement molecule
models. An oriented variable-length family exposes a particularly simple
mechanism: additional observations of a read type can reverse a likelihood
ranking without removing the structural bridging certificate.

At population read frequencies, the usual Kullback--Leibler inequality makes
the true genome a likelihood maximizer; the nontrivial question is
\emph{uniqueness}. We prove this uniqueness up to cyclic rotation for primitive
genomes satisfying a candidate-intrinsic repeat condition (P2), with the
combinatorial argument checked in Lean. The results delineate where structural
reconstruction and statistical optimization agree, and where they diverge.
Their relationship to the motivating 2016 question of Shomorony et al., citing
Medvedev and Brudno, is discussed with the relevant assumptions made explicit.
\end{abstract}
