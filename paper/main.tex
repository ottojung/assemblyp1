\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genome assembly aims to reconstruct a genome from the short DNA sequences
produced by sequencing. Under suitable conditions, the overlaps between these
sequences allow us to recover the original genome uniquely. But does this also
guarantee that the true genome is the one most likely to produce the particular
collection of sequences we observed? We investigate when this is the case
and when it fails.

We show that even when the observed pieces meet established conditions
for reconstructing the true genome, an incorrect genome can assign a
higher probability to the data. The reason is that reconstruction uses
how the pieces fit together, whereas likelihood also depends on how
often each kind of piece appears. We establish failures under exact
sequencing models, including comparisons between genomes of equal
length when observations from both DNA strands are taken into account.

We also study what happens when the genome's length is not known in
advance. We characterize how likely the observations can be under
genomes of arbitrary length and give an exact way to check whether a
proposed genome is a most likely explanation without searching through
every alternative. For some true genomes, collecting more reads does
not make unrestricted likelihood increasingly favor the truth.

On the positive side, we prove that additional information about
genome length or restrictions on repeated regions can make the true
genome distinguishable from its competitors. Under suitable conditions,
the probability that an incorrect genome ties or beats the truth
decreases exponentially as more independent reads are collected.
We also give a method that assigns a preference to shorter genomes,
always has a best candidate, and eventually recovers the true genome
under stated assumptions. These results clarify the difference
between being able to reconstruct a genome, being able to identify it
from unlimited data, and being able to recover it reliably from a
finite sample.

\end{abstract}
