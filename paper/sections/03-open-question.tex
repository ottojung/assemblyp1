\section{The question, mathematically}
\label{sec:open}

The published sentence asks whether bridging conditions can be used to
guarantee that the maximum-likelihood sequence is the true sequence
\cite[Discussion, p.~i501]{shomorony2016}. The sentence fixes no objective, no
read-type convention, no candidate class, and no tie rule, so we read it not as
one theorem awaiting one proof but as a family of precise mathematical
questions --- one per objective, read-type convention, and candidate universe ---
and we answer each member. Throughout, likelihood is a \emph{scoring function}
on candidate genomes: a counterexample is a wrong genome with a strictly higher
score, not a failed execution of an assembler. What the cited sources do and do
not determine about the sentence is audited in Appendix~\ref{app:referents}.

\subsection{The bridging hypothesis}

The hypothesis side of the question is the source's own coverage-and-bridging
predicate, quantified over observed read \emph{strings} matched at any genomic
position rather than over hidden sampled placements
\cite[Supplement, \S6.4, Definitions~1--2]{shomorony2016}.

\begin{definition}[Historical coverage and matched-string bridging]
\label{def:historical-match}
Let $S$ be a circular genome of length $G$, let $2\le L\le G$ be a read
length, and let $\mathcal{W}$ be the set of distinct observed read strings. The
predicate $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ holds if and only if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(H\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{(coverage, Definition~1)} for every start $t$ there is a string
        $w\in\mathcal{W}$ with $w=W_{(t+\delta)\bmod G}(S)$ for some
        $\delta\in\{0,1,\dots,L-2\}$;
  \item \emph{(bridging, Definition~2)} every triple repeat of $S$ has
        \emph{every} copy strictly straddled by some observed string matched at
        a position in the source's straddling interval, and every interleaved
        repeat pair has a copy so straddled.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The placement-based predicate $\Is$ of Definition~\ref{def:Is} formalizes the
same three-clause shape at the latent sampled starts. The two predicates are
incomparable, and no theorem is stated for one and silently attributed to the
other (Proposition~\ref{prop:historical-source}).

\subsection{Not-So-Greedy: structural reconstruction and its sufficient theorem}

The 2016 paper is not primarily a scoring method. Its Not-So-Greedy
construction is a \emph{structural reconstruction} procedure: from the
observed reads it builds an overlap multigraph and reads off an Eulerian
cycle, and the bridging conditions are a \emph{sufficient} theorem for that
procedure to spell the truth exactly. We record the published result as an
external premise, because the two-question distinction below turns on exactly
what it does and does not guarantee.

\begin{theorem}[Not-So-Greedy exact reconstruction; published 2016 result]
\label{rem:notso-greedy}
\claimS. Let $S$ be a circular genome and let the observed read set satisfy
the source's coverage-and-bridging predicate $\Is^{\mathrm{match}}$ of
Definition~\ref{def:historical-match} (Supplement \S6.4, Definitions~1--2).
Then the Not-So-Greedy construction produces an overlap multigraph of the
observed reads whose unique Eulerian cycle spells $S$ up to cyclic shift
 \cite[Supplement]{shomorony2016}.
\end{theorem}

Three features of this premise matter for everything that follows. First, the
conclusion is about the \emph{feasible set}: within the full fixed source
assumptions, at most one genome up to cyclic rotation satisfies the strong
guarantee for the same observations. Second, the hypothesis is much stronger
than read-generation consistency: many genomes can generate the same reads ---
Example~\ref{ex:ABAB} exhibits two with identical read distributions --- so
bridging feasibility is a strictly stronger property than inducing the same
likelihood. Third, the theorem says nothing about the likelihood ranking of
candidates outside the feasible set; that gap is the 2016 question.

\subsection{Two questions, not one}
\label{sec:two-questions}

The published sentence asks whether bridging conditions guarantee that the
maximum-likelihood sequence is the true sentence's referent. Read literally,
it names one question; mathematically, there are two, and conflating them is
the central error this paper guards against. Fix a finite candidate universe
$\cands$ containing the truth $S$, a prior $\pi$ on $\cands$, zero-one loss on
equivalence classes, and the common correctly specified observation model of
Definition~\ref{def:sampling}. Let $F(\cdot,R)$ be a \emph{feasibility
predicate}: for the 2016 question, $F(g,R)$ is the source's
coverage-and-bridging predicate $\Is^{\mathrm{match}}$ evaluated on candidate
$g$ against the observed read set $R$ --- a property of $g$ and $R$ alone.

\paragraph{Question A: the conditional question.}
Constrain the candidate set to the \emph{feasible fiber}
$\cands_F(R)=\{\,g\in\cands : F(g,R)\,\}$ and assume the truth is feasible,
$F(S,R)$. By the uniqueness content of Theorem~\ref{rem:notso-greedy}, the
only feasible candidate is the truth up to rotation: $\cands_F(R)=[S]$. The
conditional posterior $\pi(\cdot\mid R)$ restricted to $\cands_F(R)$ is
therefore a singleton on $[S]$, and \emph{any} valid conditional MAP rule ---
any rule that maximizes the conditional posterior, or equivalently the
conditional likelihood under the restricted prior --- returns the truth.
\claimP: this is largely a corollary of the reconstruction theorem plus the
definition of conditional probability. It is \emph{not} a new unconditional
ML result, and it licenses no statement about the likelihood ranking outside
the fiber. Issue~\#266 tracks the conditional-reconstruction analysis that
formalizes this fiber singleton result; the present paper adopts it as a
corollary and does not claim it as a new ML theorem.

\paragraph{Question B: the unconditional question.}
Assume only $F(S,R)$ --- the truth is feasible --- and maximize the
\emph{original, unrestricted} likelihood $L_n(\cdot)$ over the \emph{original}
universe $\cands$. The argmax need not be the truth: the strict
counterexamples of Section~\ref{sec:finite} exhibit feasible truths beaten by
infeasible competitors. \emph{This} is the genuinely nontrivial published
comparison, and it is the reading the remainder of the paper adopts when it
speaks of ``the 2016 question''.

\paragraph{The hinge.}
In every strict counterexample of Section~\ref{sec:finite}, the competitor
fails the feasibility predicate that the truth satisfies --- otherwise the
uniqueness content of Theorem~\ref{rem:notso-greedy} would be violated.
Questions A and B therefore differ in exactly one place: whether the
optimization is restricted to the feasible fiber. Inside the fiber, the truth
is the unique conditional maximizer; outside it, likelihood can and does
prefer other candidates.

\paragraph{The conditioning is truth-dependent, and an R-only algorithm need
not know it.}
The event $E=F(S,R)$ --- ``the truth is feasible'' --- is defined through the
truth, not through $R$ alone: seeing the reads, one cannot in general tell
whether the genome that generated them satisfies the bridging predicate.
Conditioning on $E$ and restricting to $\cands_F(R)$ is thus a different
experiment from the unconditional one, not a refinement available to every
likelihood algorithm. The finite kernel-checked evaluator of
issue~\#267 makes the cost exact (\claimK, \path{AssemblyP1/FiniteMapOptimality.lean}):
with two genomes, two observations, equal prior, and
$P(g_1,a)=1/4,\,P(g_1,b)=3/4,\,P(g_2,a)=3/4,\,P(g_2,b)=1/4$, the rule
$\delta_E$ that is infallible on $E=\{(g_1,a),(g_2,b)\}$ has prior-averaged
accuracy $1/4$, while the unconstrained MAP rule has accuracy $3/4$
(\texttt{map\_beats\_infallible\_on\_E}). A rule can be perfect on the
feasible fiber and still lose, on average, to the unconstrained ML/MAP rule.

\subsection{The objectives}

The conclusion side of the question is a likelihood score. Medvedev and Brudno
name two sequence-level objectives \cite[\S6.1]{medvedev2009}. The
\emph{exact multinomial} scores a circular candidate $D$ by its
candidate-intrinsic length,
\[
  \Lik_{\mathrm{ex}}(D;x)
  = \frac{n!}{\prod_w x(w)!}
    \prod_{w\,:\,\spec_L(D)(w)>0}
    \Bigl(\frac{\spec_L(D)(w)}{|D|}\Bigr)^{x(w)},
\]
with value $0$ if some observed word has zero multiplicity in $D$. The
\emph{fixed-$N$ binomial} replaces the candidate length by an external genome
size $N$, assumed known, and scores a separable product of per-type marginals
(Definition~\ref{def:likelihoods}); it is defined only where every type count
lies strictly between $0$ and $N$. Their algorithm's feasible set is a third
object, the \emph{Section~6.2 read-overlap flow}: vertices are observed DNA
molecules (a strand together with its reverse complement), edges are overlaps
of length at least $o_{\min}$, the transitively reduced graph imposes
vertex-flow lower bound $1$, and a feasible flow may decompose into several
walks rather than spell one circular genome \cite[\S6.2]{medvedev2009}. On this
object the phrase ``the maximum-likelihood sequence'' can fail to denote
anything, because the score-maximizing object may be a non-sequence flow. The
read-type convention and the genome equivalence are coupled
(Appendix~\ref{app:referents}, Table~\ref{tab:axes}): molecule read types force
dihedral equivalence, oriented read types force only cyclic shift.

\subsection{Bayes optimality of maximum likelihood, with its scope}
\label{sec:bayes}

Before the finite answer, we state the general guarantee that makes ML the
natural benchmark, together with the precise scope without which the guarantee
is false.

\begin{definition}[Decision rule and prior-averaged success]
\label{def:decision-rule}
Fix a finite candidate universe $\cands$ containing the truth $S$, a prior
$\pi$ on $\cands$, and zero-one loss on equivalence classes. A
\emph{decision rule} $\delta$ maps the observed read multiset to a candidate,
possibly at random. The \emph{prior-averaged success} of $\delta$ at sample
size $n$ is
\[
  S_n(\delta) \;=\; \sum_{G \in \cands} \pi(G)\, P_G\bigl(\delta(R_1,\dots,R_n)=G\bigr),
\]
the probability --- averaged over both the prior and the sampling process ---
that $\delta$ outputs the true genome.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Bayes optimality of maximum likelihood]
\label{rem:bayes}
Let $\cands$ be a finite candidate universe containing the truth $S$, with a
correctly specified common observation model (Definition~\ref{def:sampling}),
a uniform prior over the target equivalence classes, and zero-one loss. Then
any maximum-likelihood rule --- any maximizer of $L_n$, ties broken
arbitrarily --- maximizes $S_n(\delta)$ over all deterministic and randomized
decision rules, simultaneously for every finite $n$.
\claimT{} for the general theorem \cite[Ch.~1--2]{berger1985}; \claimK{} for
the finite exact evaluator in its arbitrary-prior MAP form
(\path{AssemblyP1/FiniteMapOptimality.lean}, \texttt{bayes\_optimality},
issue~\#267 worktree, verified building on this branch). The uniform-prior
ML specialization and the genome-scale composition are conditional pending
the kernel adapter of issue~\#269.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Decompose by the observed read multiset $w$:
$S_n(\delta)=\sum_w P(w)\,\pi(\delta(w)\mid w)$, where
$P(w)=\sum_G \pi(G)P_G(w)$ and $\pi(G\mid w)=\pi(G)P_G(w)/P(w)$ is the
posterior. Since $\pi(\delta(w)\mid w)\le \max_G \pi(G\mid w)$ pointwise, with
equality iff $\delta(w)$ is a posterior maximizer,
\[
  S_n(\delta)\;\le\; \sum_w \max_G \pi(G)P_G(w) \;=\; S_n^*,
\]
and the bound is attained by any posterior-maximizing rule. Under a uniform
prior, $\argmax_G \pi(G\mid w)=\argmax_G P_G(w)=\argmax_G L_n(G)$, so the
maximizer is the ML rule. A randomized rule is a mixture of deterministic
ones and $S_n$ is linear in the rule, so no randomized rule exceeds the best
deterministic one. \qed
\end{proof}

The guarantee is precise, and its limits are part of its content.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Prior-averaged, not per-truth.} Theorem~\ref{rem:bayes} averages
        over the prior. For a fixed truth $G$, the success probability
        $P_G(\delta=G)$ can be arbitrarily small for some truths --- a truth
        whose read distribution nearly coincides with a decoy's --- and no rule
        is uniformly optimal for every fixed truth unless $|\cands|=1$.
  \item \emph{Uniform prior and zero-one loss are used essentially.} Under a
        non-uniform prior the Bayes rule is the MAP rule for that prior, not
        ML: in the kernel-checked $2\times2$ example above, MAP selects $g_1$
        (joint weight $9/20$) where ML selects $g_2$ (likelihood $1$)
        (\texttt{map\_ne\_ml\_nonuniform}, \claimK). Under a different loss
        the Bayes rule minimizes posterior expected loss instead.
  \item \emph{Ties must be handled consistently.} When several candidates tie,
        every maximizer is Bayes-optimal for prior-averaged success, but exact
        identification \emph{within} a tie class of size $m$ is limited by
        the class size: a rule that always outputs one member of the class is
        correct on it with probability at most $1/m$. This quantifies the
        weak/strong schema distinction of Definition~\ref{def:schemas}.
  \item \emph{Rotation classes are the canonical tie class.} Rotations are
        observationally equivalent (Remark~\ref{rem:rotations-equiv}), so the
        prior is placed on rotation classes and the strong schema is the
        satisfiable one (Remark~\ref{rem:cyclic}).
  \item \emph{The MB09 binomial is not exact Bayes ML.} By
        Proposition~\ref{prop:multinomial-iid}, the exact multinomial is the
        i.i.d.\ likelihood up to a constant; the fixed-$N$ binomial is a
        separable pseudo-likelihood with an external $N$. Optimizing the
        binomial is not optimizing the Bayes rule of the sampling model.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Prior-average success is nondecreasing; per-truth success need not be]
\label{rem:mono-avg}
Prior-average success is nondecreasing in $n$ --- the Bayes-optimal
$S_{n+1}\ge S_n$ for every $n$, any prior --- because the rule that at $n+1$
reads applies the optimal $n$-read rule to the first $n$ reads achieves
$S_n$, so the optimum at $n+1$ is at least $S_n$ \claimT
\parencite{berger1985,durrett2019}. The companion statement for a \emph{fixed
truth} is false: per-truth success can drop as reads accumulate (a
kernel-checked per-truth counterexample is owned by issue~\#269; a
computed instance is recorded in the issue~\#265 worktree). This asymmetry is
the sharpest form of the paper's central distinction: the ML rule improves
\emph{on average}, never \emph{for every genome}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Finite-sample bounds: a scoped 99\% statement]
\label{rem:sample-complexity}
For a finite family of $M$ candidates whose read distributions are pairwise
distinct, the per-truth error probability of the ML rule decays
exponentially: $P_S(\text{ML errs at } n)\le \sum_{G'\ne S}\rho(P_S,P_{G'})^n
\le (M-1)\rho_{\max}^n$, where $\rho(P,Q)=\sum_w\sqrt{P(w)Q(w)}$ is the
Hellinger affinity \parencite{cover2006,tsybakov2009}. Hence
$(M-1)\rho_{\max}^n\le 0.01$ holds once
$n\ge \ln(100(M-1))/\ln(1/\rho_{\max})$: for example $M=10$, $\rho_{\max}=0.9$
gives $n\ge 65$, and $M=100$, $\rho_{\max}=0.95$ gives $n\ge 180$. The bound
needs $\rho_{\max}<1$ --- every decoy distinguishable from the truth --- and
is vacuous for rotations ($\rho=1$). \claimT{} for the bound itself;
\openstatus{} for the value of $\rho_{\max}$ in any candidate class this
project studies (all circular words of a given length, the oriented primitive
P2 class, or the MB09 \S6.2 flow domain): the limit theorem supplies no
finite $n$, and the affinity bound applies only once $\rho_{\max}$ is computed
for a finite, distinguishable family. We pose the bound as a rigorous
template and record the constants as open rather than quietly claiming an
unproved rate.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The finite answer}

At finite sample the answer is negative on every same-length slice we have been
able to formalize --- including under the source's own predicate --- and
positive only on a conditional oriented slice whose extra premises we state
openly.

\paragraph{These failures are not a paradox.}
Each witness below is a \emph{pointwise finite-sample failure}: at a fixed
truth and a fixed unrepresentative sample, the ML rule errs strictly. Such
failures are fully consistent with Theorem~\ref{rem:bayes} --- which is a
statement averaged over the prior, not a per-truth guarantee --- and with
limiting consistency (Remark~\ref{rem:sample-complexity}). They are also
consistent with Question A: in every witness, the beating competitor lies
outside the feasible fiber, so the conditional guarantee is untouched. The
finite landscape is therefore not a contradiction but a map of where each of
the five notions of Section~\ref{sec:five-notions} applies, and we preserve
each witness's exact scope --- observation model, candidate membership,
historical read-string versus placement-based coverage, candidate lengths,
prior family, and likelihood formula --- rather than reading one row as
another.

\begin{counterexample}[Strict historical counterexamples $W_1$ and $W_2$]
\label{ex:W1W2}
Let $L=3$ and let the alphabet carry the DNA involution. Under the source
predicate $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ of Definition~\ref{def:historical-match}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*]
  \item $S=AAATAT$ against $D=AAAAAT$ ($G=6$): $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ holds at
        full strength --- historical coverage, every literal three-occurrence
        triple repeat all-bridged, every interleaved pair bridged --- and $S$
        and $D$ are genuine Section~6.2 bidirected molecule flows of equal
        length at the full overlap $o_{\min}=L-1=2$; the competitor strictly
        wins,
        \[
          \Lik_{\mathrm{ex}}(D)/\Lik_{\mathrm{ex}}(S)=3,\qquad
          \Lik_{\mathrm{bin}}(D)/\Lik_{\mathrm{bin}}(S)=5,
        \]
        at external $N=6$ with the zero-count factors retained;
  \item $S=ATATACAC$ against $D=ATACACAC$ ($G=8$): the same scope, additionally
        per-occurrence feasible, with exact ratio $3/2$ and binomial ratio
        $9/5$ at $N=8$.
\end{enumerate}
Both inequalities are strict, so both the maximizer and uniqueness schemas fail
under every equivalence and tie convention.
\end{counterexample}

Coordinate-level spectra, the flow certificates, and the kernel-checked Lean
endpoints are in Section~\ref{sec:finite:historical}
(Theorems~\ref{rem:cex-historical-W1}--\ref{rem:cex-historical-W2}). The failure is not an artifact of
the flow restriction: $AAABB\to AAAAB$ beats the exact multinomial by $2$ and
$AAACC\to AAAAC$ beats the fixed-$N$ binomial by $1125/512$, both under full
$\Is$ at the realized placements
(Theorems~\ref{rem:cex-exact}--\ref{rem:cex-binomial}), and each same-length
witness transfers to every superclass of candidates
(Lemma~\ref{lem:negative-transfer}).

The positive statement is conditional, and its premises are strict and
project-level rather than the source's.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional same-length rigidity and uniqueness]
\label{thm:open-rigidity}
Let $S$ be a circular truth of length $G$, let the realized read placement set
satisfy the project's sampled-placement $\Is$, and suppose the truth is a genuine
Section~6.2 candidate of the observed reads. Among same-length genuine
Section~6.2 candidates with \emph{oriented} read types, the truth is the unique
maximizer of both the exact multinomial and the fixed-$N$ binomial at $N=G$, up
to cyclic rotation.
\end{theorem}

Full statement, proof, and kernel-checked status: Section~\ref{sec:finite}
(Theorems~\ref{thm:maximizer} and~\ref{rem:rigidity},
Corollary~\ref{rem:same-length-rotation}). The premises are exactly: oriented (not
molecule) read types, the placement-based $\Is$ (not $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$),
equal candidate length, and truth admissibility. Dropping truth candidacy makes
the statement false at equal length ($AABB\to ABAB$); dropping equal length
restores strict counterexamples in infinite families
(Theorem~\ref{rem:cex-variable-length}). No strict same-length counterexample
exists for any $G$, $L$, or alphabet on this row.

\subsection{The population answer}

Replacing empirical read frequencies by the exact population read distribution
removes the sampling mechanism. A Gibbs/KL identity then makes the truth a
population maximizer over \emph{every} candidate class with no repeat hypothesis,
and uniqueness reduces to spectrum identifiability.

\begin{theorem}[Population uniqueness]
\label{thm:open-population}
For $L\ge2$, the true genome $S$ is a population maximum-likelihood genome over
every candidate class containing it (Lemma~\ref{rem:gibbs}); among
primitive P2-admissible oriented circular candidates it is the unique such
maximizer up to cyclic rotation (Theorem~\ref{rem:population}).
\end{theorem}

\sloppy
Both directions are kernel-checked with no external premise:
\popml. This repaired population model does not settle the finite 2016
question; it isolates the identifiability content that the finite
counterexamples show is not automatic.

\subsection{Five notions, a worked example, and the theorem/assumption matrix}
\label{sec:five-notions}

Five properties of the ML rule circulate in this literature. They are
independent, and conflating any two produces a false headline.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Conditional guaranteed reconstruction} (Question A): given
        $F(S,R)$, the feasible fiber is a singleton and any valid conditional
        MAP returns the truth. A corollary of the reconstruction theorem;
        silent about the unrestricted ranking. \claimP/\claimS
  \item \textbf{Prior-average Bayes optimality}: with a uniform prior over
        equivalence classes and zero-one loss on a finite universe, ML
        maximizes prior-averaged success at every $n$. \claimK{} (finite
        core) + \claimT
  \item \textbf{Pointwise finite-sample failure}: at a fixed truth and
        unrepresentative sample, ML can err strictly. The counterexamples of
        Section~\ref{sec:finite}. \claimK
  \item \textbf{Identifiability}: distinct candidates have distinct read
        distributions. Fails in general (Example~\ref{ex:ABAB}); proved for
        the oriented primitive P2 class (Section~\ref{sec:population}).
        \claimK{} for the P2 class
  \item \textbf{Limiting consistency}: for a finite identifiable family,
        per-truth success tends to $1$ as $n\to\infty$. An external theorem
        about finite families; distinct from the population theorem, which
        involves no sampling and allows arbitrarily large candidate classes.
        \claimT
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}[One $2\times2$ universe, all five notions]
\label{ex:five-notions}
Take two genomes $g_1,g_2$, two observations $a,b$, equal prior $1/2$, and
$P(g_1,a)=1/4$, $P(g_1,b)=3/4$, $P(g_2,a)=3/4$, $P(g_2,b)=1/4$; reads are
i.i.d.\ All arithmetic below is exact and kernel-checked
(\path{AssemblyP1/FiniteMapOptimality.lean}, issue~\#267 worktree).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item the two genomes are \emph{identifiable}: their read distributions
        differ, so consistency applies --- the MAP rule selects the truth with
        probability tending to $1$;
  \item the event $E=\{(g_1,a),(g_2,b)\}$ --- the truth is ``feasible'' in the
        toy sense --- has prior probability $1/4$; the rule $\delta_E$ that is
        infallible on $E$ is a \emph{conditional guaranteed reconstruction}:
        perfect on the fiber;
  \item yet $\delta_E$ has prior-averaged accuracy exactly $1/4$, while the
        unconstrained MAP rule has accuracy $3/4$ --- \emph{prior-average
        Bayes optimality} prefers MAP, not the fiber-perfect rule;
  \item off $E$, $\delta_E$ errs --- a \emph{pointwise failure} of the
        conditional rule, with no contradiction anywhere.
\end{itemize}
The assembly-level counterpart is Example~\ref{ex:frequencies} and
Theorem~\ref{rem:cex-exact}: there the fiber-perfect intuition is the
conditional guarantee of Theorem~\ref{rem:rigidity}, the pointwise
failure is the strict ratio $2$, and prior-average optimality is untouched.
\end{example}

\subsection{What remains open}

Three items stay open and are settled by no theorem above: which of the three
objectives --- or which broader maximum-likelihood principle --- the sentence
denotes (Appendix~\ref{app:referents}); the general-$o_{\min}$ Section~6.2 flow
domain below full overlap, where the score-maximizing object can be a
non-sequence flow (Remark~\ref{rem:flow-domain}); and the population model
for molecule read types. Two further residues are recorded in the matrix
(Table~\ref{tab:claims}): the genome-scale composition of the Bayes-optimality
theorem (kernel adapter owned by issue~\#269) and the 99\% sample-size
constants for the project's candidate classes.
