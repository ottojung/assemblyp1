\section{The question, mathematically}
\label{sec:open}

The 2016 Not-So-Greedy theorem turns sufficiently informative reads into
a unique Eulerian reconstruction of a circular genome. The likelihood
question is what happens if, instead of following the reconstruction graph,
we rank candidate genomes by the probability they assign to the
\emph{observed counts} of each read type. In this section we give the
bridging and likelihood definitions, state a positive same-length result,
exhibit exact finite likelihood reversals, and show why the population
version admits a stronger uniqueness theorem.

Shomorony et al.\ explicitly asked whether bridging can guarantee that
the maximum-likelihood sequence is the true sequence
\cite[Discussion, p.~i501]{shomorony2016}. The answer depends on the
chosen likelihood objective and admissible candidate class; the precise
source-to-model correspondence is retained in
Appendix~\ref{app:referents}.

\subsection{The bridging hypothesis}

The hypothesis side of the question is the source's own coverage-and-bridging
predicate, quantified over observed read \emph{strings} matched at any genomic
position rather than over hidden sampled placements
\cite[Supplement, \S6.4, Definitions~1--2]{shomorony2016}.

\begin{definition}[Historical coverage and matched-string bridging]
\label{def:historical-match}
Let $S$ be a circular genome of length $G$, let $2\le L\le G$ be a read
length, and let $\mathcal{W}$ be the set of distinct observed read strings. The
predicate $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ holds if and only if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(H\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{(coverage, Definition~1)} for every start $t$ there is a string
        $w\in\mathcal{W}$ with $w=W_{(t+\delta)\bmod G}(S)$ for some
        $\delta\in\{0,1,\dots,L-2\}$;
  \item \emph{(bridging, Definition~2)} every triple repeat of $S$ has
        \emph{every} copy strictly straddled by some observed string matched at
        a position in the source's straddling interval, and every interleaved
        repeat pair has a copy so straddled.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The placement-based predicate $\Is$ of Definition~\ref{def:Is} formalizes the
same three-clause shape at the latent sampled starts. The two predicates are
incomparable, and no theorem is stated for one and silently attributed to the
other (Proposition~\ref{prop:historical-source}).

\subsection{Not-So-Greedy as structural reconstruction}

The 2016 paper is not primarily a scoring method. Its Not-So-Greedy construction
is a \emph{structural reconstruction} procedure: from the observed reads it
builds an overlap multigraph, removes spurious edges, adjusts edge
multiplicities, and reads off an Eulerian cycle. The bridging conditions are a
\emph{sufficient} theorem for that procedure to spell the truth exactly, and we
record the published result as an external premise, because the two-question
distinction below turns on exactly what it does and does not guarantee.

\begin{theorem}[Not-So-Greedy exact reconstruction; published 2016 result]
\label{thm:notso-greedy}
\srcfact{}. Let $S$ be a circular genome and suppose the observed read set
satisfies the source's coverage-and-bridging predicate
$I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ of Definition~\ref{def:historical-match}
(Supplement~\S6.4, Definitions~1--2). Then the Not-So-Greedy construction
produces an overlap multigraph of the observed reads whose unique Eulerian
cycle spells $S$ up to cyclic shift \cite[Supplement]{shomorony2016}.
\end{theorem}

Three features of this premise matter for everything that follows. First, its
conclusion is about the \emph{feasible set}: within the full fixed source
assumptions, at most one genome up to cyclic rotation satisfies the strong
guarantee for the same observations. Second, its hypothesis is much stronger
than read-generation consistency: many genomes generate the \emph{same} reads
--- Example~\ref{ex:ABAB} exhibits two with identical read distributions --- so
bridging feasibility is a strictly stronger property than inducing the same
likelihood, and the theorem is \emph{not} the claim ``only one candidate genome
can generate the reads''. Third, the theorem says nothing about the likelihood
ranking of candidates outside the feasible set; that gap is the 2016 question.

\subsection{Two questions, not one}
\label{sec:two-questions}

Read literally, the published sentence names one question; mathematically there
are two, and conflating them is the central error this paper guards against.
Fix a finite candidate universe $\cands$ containing the truth $S$, a prior
$\pi$ on $\cands$, zero-one loss on equivalence classes, and the common
correctly specified observation model of Definitions~\ref{def:reads}
and~\ref{def:likelihoods}. Let $F(\cdot,R)$ be a \emph{feasibility predicate}:
for the 2016 question, $F(g,R)$ is the source's coverage-and-bridging predicate
$I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ evaluated on candidate $g$ against the observed read set
$R$ --- a property of $g$ and $R$ alone.

\paragraph{Question A: the conditional question.}
Constrain the candidate set to the \emph{feasible fiber}
$\cands_F(R)=\{\,g\in\cands : F(g,R)\,\}$ and assume the truth is feasible,
$F(S,R)$. By the uniqueness content of Theorem~\ref{thm:notso-greedy}, the only
feasible candidate is the truth up to rotation: $\cands_F(R)=[S]$. The
conditional posterior $\pi(\cdot\mid R)$ restricted to $\cands_F(R)$ is
therefore a singleton on $[S]$, and \emph{any} valid conditional MAP rule ---
any rule maximizing the conditional posterior, equivalently the conditional
likelihood under the restricted prior --- returns the truth. This is largely a
corollary of the reconstruction theorem plus the definition of conditional
probability; it is \emph{not} a new unconditional ML result and licenses no
statement about the likelihood ranking outside the fiber. Issue~\#266 tracks the
conditional-reconstruction analysis that formalizes this fiber-singleton result;
the present paper adopts it as a corollary and does not claim it as a new ML
theorem.

\paragraph{Question B: the unconditional question.}
Assume only $F(S,R)$ --- the truth is feasible --- and maximize the
\emph{original, unrestricted} likelihood $\Lik$ over the \emph{original}
universe $\cands$. The maximizer need not be the truth: the strict
counterexamples of Section~\ref{sec:finite} exhibit feasible truths beaten by
competitors outside the fiber. \emph{This} is the genuinely nontrivial published
comparison, and it is the reading the rest of the paper adopts when it speaks of
``the 2016 question''.

\paragraph{The hinge.}
In every strict counterexample of Section~\ref{sec:finite} the competitor fails
the feasibility predicate the truth satisfies --- otherwise the uniqueness
content of Theorem~\ref{thm:notso-greedy} would be violated. Questions A and B
therefore differ in exactly one place: whether the optimization is restricted to
the feasible fiber. Inside the fiber the truth is the unique conditional
maximizer; outside it, likelihood can and does prefer other candidates.

\paragraph{The conditioning is truth-dependent.}
The event $E=F(S,R)$ --- ``the truth is feasible'' --- is defined through the
truth, not through $R$ alone: seeing the reads, one cannot in general tell
whether the genome that generated them satisfies the bridging predicate.
Conditioning on $E$ and restricting to $\cands_F(R)$ is therefore a different
experiment from the unconditional one, not a refinement available to every
likelihood algorithm. An algorithm that sees only $R$ need not know whether $E$
holds. A fiber-perfect rule can be worse than the unconstrained ML rule on
prior-averaged success (worked Example~\ref{ex:five-notions} below).

\subsection{The objectives}

The conclusion side of the question is a likelihood score. Medvedev and Brudno
name two sequence-level objectives \cite[\S6.1]{medvedev2009}. The
\emph{exact multinomial} scores a circular candidate $D$ by its
candidate-intrinsic length,
\[
  \Lik_{\mathrm{ex}}(D;x)
  = \frac{n!}{\prod_w x(w)!}
    \prod_{w\,:\,\spec_L(D)(w)>0}
    \Bigl(\frac{\spec_L(D)(w)}{|D|}\Bigr)^{x(w)},
\]
with value $0$ if some observed word has zero multiplicity in $D$. The
\emph{fixed-$N$ binomial} replaces the candidate length by an external genome
size $N$, assumed known, and scores a separable product of per-type marginals
(Definition~\ref{def:likelihoods}); it is defined only where every type count
lies strictly between $0$ and $N$. Their algorithm's feasible set is a third
object, the \emph{Section~6.2 read-overlap flow}: vertices are observed DNA
molecules (a strand together with its reverse complement), edges are overlaps
of length at least $o_{\min}$, the transitively reduced graph imposes
vertex-flow lower bound $1$, and a feasible flow may decompose into several
walks rather than spell one circular genome \cite[\S6.2]{medvedev2009}. On this
object the phrase ``the maximum-likelihood sequence'' can fail to denote
anything, because the score-maximizing object may be a non-sequence flow. The
read-type convention and the genome equivalence are coupled
(Appendix~\ref{app:referents}, Table~\ref{tab:axes}): molecule read types force
dihedral equivalence, oriented read types force only cyclic shift.

\subsection{Why likelihood? Optimality as a decision rule}
\label{sec:bayes-rationale}

The attraction of maximum likelihood is not merely that it supplies a numerical
score. Under a correct statistical model it is an optimal reconstruction rule
for a precise and important notion of correctness probability.

\begin{theorem}[Bayes-optimal reconstruction; ML under a uniform prior]
\label{thm:bayes-optimal}
Let $\Theta$ be a nonempty finite set of possible true genomes (with any
appropriate rotation or reverse-complement equivalence already incorporated),
and let $X$ be a finite set of possible read observations. Fix a prior
$\pi(\theta)$ and a conditional sampling distribution
$P(x\mid\theta)$. Let $\widehat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(x)$ maximize
$\pi(\theta)P(x\mid\theta)$. For every deterministic or randomized
reconstruction algorithm $A$,
\[
  \Pr_{\theta\sim\pi,\;x\sim P(\cdot\mid\theta)}
       [A(x)=\theta]
  \ \le\
  \Pr_{\theta\sim\pi,\;x\sim P(\cdot\mid\theta)}
       [\widehat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(x)=\theta].
\]
For a uniform prior, MAP is maximum likelihood, so ML is optimal by this
same correctness-probability criterion. This holds for every finite sample
size, without requiring coverage or repeat-bridging conditions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $J(\theta,x)=\pi(\theta)P(x\mid\theta)$. For a deterministic
decision $d$, its total success probability is
$\sum_x J(d(x),x)$, which is at most
$\sum_x \max_\theta J(\theta,x)$ by pointwise maximization. For a
randomized rule with output weights $q(\theta\mid x)\ge0$ summing to one,
its success probability is
\[
  \sum_x\sum_\theta q(\theta\mid x)J(\theta,x)
  \le \sum_x\max_\theta J(\theta,x).
\]
A MAP choice attains the right-hand side at every $x$. A uniform prior
does not change the maximizers of $P(x\mid\theta)$.
\end{proof}

\statusline{\kernelcheck{} for the finite deterministic and randomized
decision inequalities, the existence of a finite MAP maximizer, and the
iid finite-observation specialization:
\texttt{AssemblyP1/FiniteBayesDecision.lean}
(\texttt{chooseMAP\_optimal},
\texttt{chooseMAP\_optimal\_randomized},
\texttt{finite\_iid\_MAP\_optimal}). This is a formalization of a
standard decision-theoretic result, not a new assembly theorem.}

The theorem compares \emph{average} recovery probabilities over the stated
prior and the sampling experiment. It makes no claim that ML has the highest
success probability separately for every fixed true genome, nor that any
finite observation uniquely identifies its source. Nor does it confer this
guarantee on the independent-binomial surrogate of
Definition~\ref{def:likelihoods}: that surrogate need not rank genomes like
the true joint distribution. Thus Bayes optimality is the \emph{entrance}
to our investigation, not its central answer. The next sections study the
substantial, assembly-specific questions of when the true genome is a
maximizer for a realized read sample, when a counterexample exists, and when
the read distribution determines the genome uniquely.

The guarantee is precise, and its limits are part of its content.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Prior-averaged, not per-truth.} The average is over the prior and
        the sampling experiment. For a fixed truth $G$, the success probability
        can be arbitrarily small when $G$'s read distribution nearly coincides
        with a decoy's; no rule is uniformly optimal for every fixed truth
        unless $|\cands|=1$.
  \item \emph{Uniform prior and zero-one loss are used essentially.} Under a
        nonuniform prior the Bayes rule is the MAP rule for that prior, not ML;
        under a different loss it minimizes posterior expected loss instead.
        Uniformity is sufficient for the maximizer sets to coincide, but it is a
        hypothesis of the theorem, not a consequence of it.
  \item \emph{Rotation classes are the canonical tie class.} Rotations are
        observationally equivalent (Remark~\ref{rem:cyclic}), so the prior is
        placed on rotation classes and the strong schema of
        Definition~\ref{def:schemas} is the satisfiable one. Within a tie class
        of size $m$, exact identification is limited to probability $1/m$.
  \item \emph{The MB09 binomial is not exact Bayes ML.} The fixed-$N$ binomial
        of Definition~\ref{def:likelihoods} is a separable pseudo-likelihood
        with an external $N$; optimizing it is not optimizing the Bayes rule of
        the sampling model. Section~\ref{sec:open} keeps the objectives
        distinct.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Prior-average success is nondecreasing; per-truth monotonicity is separate]
\label{rem:mono-avg}
Prior-average success is nondecreasing in $n$ --- the Bayes-optimal value at
$n+1$ is at least its value at $n$, for every prior --- because the rule that at
$n+1$ reads applies the optimal $n$-read rule to the first $n$ reads achieves
the $n$-read optimum \cite{berger1985,durrett2019}. The kernel-checked finite
statement is \texttt{chooseMAP\_\allowbreak success\_\allowbreak monotone\_\allowbreak under\_\allowbreak refinement}
in \texttt{AssemblyP1/\allowbreak FiniteBayesDecision.\allowbreak lean}. No corresponding per-truth
monotonicity is asserted: the theorem is prior-averaged, and the finite
counterexamples of Section~\ref{sec:finite} are precisely pointwise failures at
a fixed truth. Separating the two is the paper's central distinction.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A scoped $99\%$ finite-sample bound]
\label{rem:sample-complexity}
For a finite family of $M$ candidates whose read distributions are pairwise
distinct, the per-truth error probability of the ML rule decays exponentially:
\[
  P_S(\text{ML errs at } n)\;\le\;\sum_{G'\ne S}\rho(P_S,P_{G'})^n
  \;\le\;(M-1)\,\rho_{\max}^{\,n},
\]
where $\rho(P,Q)=\sum_w\sqrt{P(w)Q(w)}$ is the Hellinger affinity
\cite{cover2006,tsybakov2009}. Hence $(M-1)\rho_{\max}^{\,n}\le 0.01$ holds once
$n\ge \ln(100(M-1))/\ln(1/\rho_{\max})$; for example $M=10$, $\rho_{\max}=0.9$
gives $n\ge 65$, and $M=100$, $\rho_{\max}=0.95$ gives $n\ge 180$. The bound
needs $\rho_{\max}<1$ --- every decoy distinguishable from the truth --- and is
vacuous across rotations ($\rho=1$). The inequality is a textbook result; the
value of $\rho_{\max}$ for any candidate class studied here (all circular words
of a given length, the oriented primitive P2 class, or the MB09~\S6.2 flow
domain) is \emph{open}, so no finite rate for the project's own classes is
claimed. We state the template and record the constants as open rather than
quietly asserting an unproved rate.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The finite answer}

At finite sample, several natural same-length versions of the implication
are false, including under the source's own bridging predicate. A more
restricted oriented slice is positive under explicit extra premises.
These are not contradictory statements: the candidate class, read-type
convention, and bridging predicate differ.

\paragraph{These failures are not a paradox.}
Each witness below is a \emph{pointwise finite-sample failure}: at a fixed truth
and a fixed unrepresentative sample, the ML rule errs strictly. Such failures
are fully consistent with the prior-averaged Bayes guarantee of
Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal} --- which averages over the prior, not over a
single truth --- and with limiting consistency for an identifiable finite family
(Remark~\ref{rem:sample-complexity}). They are also consistent with Question~A
of Section~\ref{sec:two-questions}: in every witness the beating competitor lies
outside the feasible fiber, so the conditional guarantee is untouched. The
finite landscape is therefore not a contradiction but a map of where each of the
five notions of Section~\ref{sec:five-notions} applies, and we preserve each
witness's exact scope --- observation model, candidate membership, historical
read-string versus placement-based coverage, candidate lengths, prior family,
and likelihood formula --- rather than reading one row as another.

\begin{counterexample}[Strict historical counterexamples $W_1$ and $W_2$]
\label{ex:W1W2}
Let $L=3$ and let the alphabet carry the DNA involution. Under the source
predicate $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ of Definition~\ref{def:historical-match}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*]
  \item $S=AAATAT$ against $D=AAAAAT$ ($G=6$): $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ holds at
        full strength --- historical coverage, every literal three-occurrence
        triple repeat all-bridged, every interleaved pair bridged --- and $S$
        and $D$ are genuine Section~6.2 bidirected molecule flows of equal
        length at the full overlap $o_{\min}=L-1=2$; the competitor strictly
        wins,
        \[
          \Lik_{\mathrm{ex}}(D)/\Lik_{\mathrm{ex}}(S)=3,\qquad
          \Lik_{\mathrm{bin}}(D)/\Lik_{\mathrm{bin}}(S)=5,
        \]
        at external $N=6$ with the zero-count factors retained;
  \item $S=ATATACAC$ against $D=ATACACAC$ ($G=8$): the same scope, additionally
        per-occurrence feasible, with exact ratio $3/2$ and binomial ratio
        $9/5$ at $N=8$.
\end{enumerate}
Both inequalities are strict, so both the maximizer and uniqueness schemas fail
under every equivalence and tie convention.
\end{counterexample}

Coordinate-level spectra, the flow certificates, and the kernel-checked Lean
endpoints are in Section~\ref{sec:finite:historical}
(Theorems~\ref{thm:cex-historical-W1}--\ref{thm:cex-historical-W2}). The failure is not an artifact of
the flow restriction: $AAABB\to AAAAB$ beats the exact multinomial by $2$ and
$AAACC\to AAAAC$ beats the fixed-$N$ binomial by $1125/512$, both under full
$\Is$ at the realized placements
(Theorems~\ref{thm:cex-exact}--\ref{thm:cex-binomial}), and each same-length
witness transfers to every superclass of candidates
(Lemma~\ref{lem:negative-transfer}).

The positive statement is conditional, and its premises are strict and
project-level rather than the source's.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional same-length rigidity and uniqueness]
\label{thm:open-rigidity}
Let $S$ be a circular truth of length $G$, let the realized read placement set
satisfy the project's sampled-placement $\Is$, and suppose the truth is a genuine
Section~6.2 candidate of the observed reads. Among same-length genuine
Section~6.2 candidates with \emph{oriented} read types, the truth is the unique
maximizer of both the exact multinomial and the fixed-$N$ binomial at $N=G$, up
to cyclic rotation.
\end{theorem}

Full statement, proof, and kernel-checked status: Section~\ref{sec:finite}
(Theorems~\ref{thm:maximizer} and~\ref{thm:rigidity},
Corollary~\ref{cor:same-length-rotation}). The premises are exactly: oriented (not
molecule) read types, the placement-based $\Is$ (not $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$),
equal candidate length, and truth admissibility. Dropping truth candidacy makes
the statement false at equal length ($AABB\to ABAB$); dropping equal length
restores strict counterexamples in infinite families
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-variable-length}). No strict same-length counterexample
exists for any $G$, $L$, or alphabet on this row.

\subsection{The population answer}

Replacing empirical read frequencies by the exact population read distribution
removes the sampling mechanism. A Gibbs/KL identity then makes the truth a
population maximizer over \emph{every} candidate class with no repeat hypothesis,
and uniqueness reduces to spectrum identifiability.

\begin{theorem}[Population uniqueness]
\label{thm:open-population}
For $L\ge2$, the true genome $S$ is a population maximum-likelihood genome over
every candidate class containing it (Lemma~\ref{lem:gibbs}); among
primitive P2-admissible oriented circular candidates it is the unique such
maximizer up to cyclic rotation (Theorem~\ref{thm:population}).
\end{theorem}

\sloppy
Both directions are kernel-checked with no external premise:
\popml. This repaired population model does not settle the finite 2016
question; it isolates the identifiability content that the finite
counterexamples show is not automatic.

\subsection{Five notions, a worked example, and the theorem/assumption matrix}
\label{sec:five-notions}

Five properties of the ML rule circulate in this literature. They are
independent, and conflating any two produces a false headline.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Conditional guaranteed reconstruction} (Question~A): given
        $F(S,R)$, the feasible fiber is a singleton up to rotation and any
        valid conditional MAP returns the truth. A corollary of the
        reconstruction theorem; silent about the unrestricted ranking.
  \item \textbf{Prior-average Bayes optimality}: with a uniform prior over
        equivalence classes and zero-one loss on a finite universe, ML
        maximizes prior-averaged success at every $n$
        (Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal}).
  \item \textbf{Pointwise finite-sample failure}: at a fixed truth and an
        unrepresentative sample, ML can err strictly
        (Section~\ref{sec:finite}).
  \item \textbf{Identifiability}: distinct candidates have distinct read
        distributions. It fails in general (Example~\ref{ex:ABAB}) and is
        proved for the oriented primitive P2 class
        (Section~\ref{sec:population}).
  \item \textbf{Limiting consistency}: for a finite identifiable family,
        per-truth success tends to $1$ as $n\to\infty$. This is an external
        theorem about finite families (Remark~\ref{rem:sample-complexity}),
        distinct from the population theorem, which involves no sampling and
        allows arbitrarily large candidate classes
        (Remark~\ref{rem:consistency-distinction}).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}[One $2\times2$ universe, all five notions]
\label{ex:five-notions}
Take two genomes $g_1,g_2$, two observations $a,b$, equal prior $1/2$, and
$P(g_1,a)=1/4$, $P(g_1,b)=3/4$, $P(g_2,a)=3/4$, $P(g_2,b)=1/4$; reads are
i.i.d. All arithmetic below is exact and instantiates the kernel-checked finite
MAP-optimality theorem (\texttt{chooseMAP\_\allowbreak optimal} in
\texttt{AssemblyP1/\allowbreak FiniteBayesDecision.\allowbreak lean}) at this family.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item the two genomes are \emph{identifiable}: their read distributions
        differ, so limiting consistency applies and the ML rule selects the
        truth with probability tending to $1$;
  \item the event $E=\{(g_1,a),(g_2,b)\}$ --- the truth is ``feasible'' in this
        toy sense --- has prior probability $1/4$; the rule that is infallible
        on $E$ is a \emph{conditional guaranteed reconstruction}, perfect on
        the fiber;
  \item yet that rule has prior-averaged accuracy exactly $1/4$, while the
        unconstrained ML rule has accuracy $3/4$: \emph{prior-average Bayes
        optimality} prefers ML, not the fiber-perfect rule;
  \item off $E$ the conditional rule errs --- a \emph{pointwise failure} with
        no contradiction anywhere.
\end{itemize}
The assembly-level counterpart is Example~\ref{ex:frequencies} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-exact}: there the fiber-perfect intuition is the
conditional guarantee of Theorem~\ref{thm:rigidity}, the pointwise failure is
the strict ratio $2$, and prior-average optimality is untouched.
\end{example}

\subsection{What remains open}

Three items stay open and are settled by no theorem above: which of the three
objectives --- or which broader maximum-likelihood principle --- the sentence
denotes (Appendix~\ref{app:referents}); the general-$o_{\min}$ Section~6.2 flow
domain below full overlap, where the score-maximizing object can be a
non-sequence flow (Remark~\ref{rem:flow-domain}); and the population model
for molecule read types. Two further residues are recorded in the matrix
(Table~\ref{tab:claims}): the $99\%$ sample-size constants $\rho_{\max}$ for the
project's own candidate classes, and per-truth (as opposed to prior-averaged)
monotonicity in $n$.
