\section{The reconstruction question and the likelihood question}
\label{sec:open}

Shomorony et al.\ ask whether their bridging conditions ensure that the
maximum-likelihood sequence is the true sequence
\parencite[Discussion]{shomorony2016}. The answer depends on which candidates and
read types the likelihood compares. The distinction between a reconstruction
guarantee and a likelihood ranking is particularly important.

\subsection{Historical bridging and reconstruction}

\begin{definition}[Historical coverage and matched-string bridging]
\label{def:historical-match}
Let $S$ be a circular genome of length $G$, let $2\le L\le G$, and let
$\mathcal W$ be the set of distinct observed read strings. The historical
predicate $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}(S,\mathcal W)$ requires:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(H\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
  \item for every start $t$, \emph{there exists}
  $\delta\in\{0,\ldots,L-2\}$ and $w\in\mathcal W$
  such that $w=W_{(t+\delta)\bmod G}(S)$;
  \item every copy of each triple repeat is strictly straddled by a matched
  observed read string, and of any two interleaved repeats at least one is
  bridged, as in the source's Definition~2.
\end{enumerate}
The source quantifies over \emph{matching read strings}, not hidden sampled
start positions \parencite[Supplement, \S6.4, Definitions~1--2]{shomorony2016}.
Clause (H1) is overlap-style coverage: it requires a matching
observed read start inside every length-$(L-1)$ start window, whereas
the placement-based coverage clause (C1) only requires sampled
read intervals to cover genome bases. In particular, (C1) alone
does not imply (H1).
The sampled-placement predicate $\Is$ in Definition~\ref{def:Is} is different;
the two are incomparable (Proposition~\ref{prop:historical-source}).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Not-So-Greedy reconstruction; published result]
\label{thm:notso-greedy}
Under the source's full assumptions, if the observed reads satisfy
$I_s^{\mathrm{match}}(S,\mathcal W)$, the Not-So-Greedy overlap-graph
construction has a unique Eulerian spelling equal to $S$ up to rotation
\parencite{shomorony2016}.
\end{theorem}

This is a structural reconstruction theorem, not an assertion that no other
genome can produce the reads. A candidate with positive sampling probability
for the data can fail the historical bridging predicate. Two multiplicities
must be kept apart. \emph{Generative} multiplicity counts the genomes that can
produce the observed reads with positive probability: the whole rotation class
always can, and nonrotational genomes can as well whenever the read law fails
to separate them (Example~\ref{ex:ABAB} is the extreme case of identical read
laws). \emph{Certificate} multiplicity counts the rotation classes on which the
source's strong guarantee holds for those same observations, and the
reconstruction theorem forces this to be a singleton: the construction is a
function of the observed reads alone, so its output is fixed by the data.

\begin{proposition}[The strong guarantee is a property of the observations]
\label{prop:strong-guarantee-singleton}
Fix an observed read set $R$ with distinct read strings $\mathcal W$, and
suppose the Not-So-Greedy construction applied to $R$ has a unique Eulerian
spelling up to rotation; write $[G^\ast(R)]$ for that class. If a circular
genome $g$ satisfies the source's full model assumptions and
$I_s^{\mathrm{match}}(g,\mathcal W)$, then $[g]=[G^\ast(R)]$. Consequently at
most one rotation class satisfies the strong guarantee for the same
observations.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The construction is a deterministic function of $\mathcal W$ alone, and its
unique Eulerian spelling up to rotation is $[G^\ast(R)]$. Applying the source
theorem with $g$ as the truth identifies $[G^\ast(R)]$ with $[g]$. Hence any
two genomes satisfying the predicate have equal rotation classes.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Two different statistical questions}
\label{sec:two-questions}

Let $\mathcal C$ be a finite set of candidate genome rotation classes containing
$S$, with prior $\pi(g)$, and let $r$ be the observed data.
Write $F(g,r)$ for the historical bridging predicate evaluated on $g$ against
the observed read strings. The precise conditional experiment has weights
\[
  \Pr(g,r,F) \;=\;
  \pi(g)\,P_g(r)\,\mathbf 1_{\{F(g,r)\}}.
\]
These weights encode \emph{both} structural feasibility and the requirement
that the candidate could generate the reads. It would be incorrect to infer
from the reconstruction theorem that the bare predicate $F(g,r)$ has a
singleton fiber: a genome inconsistent with an observed read may satisfy $F$.

\paragraph{Question A (conditional).}
Condition on $E=\{F(S,R)\}$ in the generative experiment. For a realized
$r$ of positive conditional probability, restrict posterior support to
\[
 \mathcal C^+(r)
 =\{g\in\mathcal C:\pi(g)P_g(r)>0,\ F(g,r)\}.
\]
If every $g\in\mathcal C^+(r)$ satisfies the source theorem's model
assumptions, Not-So-Greedy applied to those very reads spells each such $g$.
Its deterministic output is unique, hence $\mathcal C^+(r)\subseteq[S]$.
As $S$ has positive conditional weight on its realized event, the support is
exactly the truth's rotation class. Every MAP rule for this conditional model
therefore reconstructs that class. This conclusion relies on
$P_g(r)>0$ and is \emph{not} an unrestricted likelihood result.

\paragraph{Question B (unconditional).}
Assume $F(S,r)$ and compare $P_D(r)$ with $P_S(r)$ across the original
unrestricted candidate universe, without multiplying by $\mathbf 1_F$.
The truth can lose strictly, including under the source's own matched-string
bridging predicate. This is the substantive finite-sample question addressed
by Section~\ref{sec:finite}. The event $E$ depends on the unknown truth and
must not be treated as an observable filter available to ordinary ML.

\subsection{Why study maximum likelihood?}
\label{sec:bayes-rationale}

The optimality of ML under a uniform prior motivates the comparison, but
does not prove that it selects the true genome for each sample.

\begin{theorem}[Bayes-optimal reconstruction; ML under a uniform prior]
\label{thm:bayes-optimal}
Let $\Theta$ be a nonempty finite set of candidate equivalence classes ---
for us, cyclic-rotation classes of circular genomes --- let $X$ be a finite
observation space, let $\pi$ be a prior on $\Theta$, and let $P(x\mid\theta)$
be the correct sampling model. Under \emph{zero-one loss}, in which a rule
succeeds exactly when it returns the truth's class, a rule choosing
$\widehat\theta_{\rm MAP}(x)\in\arg\max_\theta\pi(\theta)P(x\mid\theta)$
maximizes the prior-averaged probability of exact reconstruction among
all deterministic and randomized rules. If instead $\pi$ is uniform on the
equivalence classes, MAP and maximum likelihood coincide. When the observation
is $n$ independent reads from a common model, the conclusion holds at every
finite sample size $n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $J(\theta,x)=\pi(\theta)P(x\mid\theta)$. Any randomized rule has
conditional decision weights $q(\theta\mid x)$ and success probability
\[
 \sum_x\sum_\theta q(\theta\mid x)J(\theta,x)
 \le \sum_x\max_\theta J(\theta,x),
\]
with equality for MAP. Deterministic rules are the special case in which
$q(\cdot\mid x)$ is a point mass. A uniform prior does not alter the
likelihood maximizers.
\end{proof}



\begin{remark}[The converse, tie handling, and posterior certainty]
\label{rem:bayes-certificate}
Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal} is an exact \emph{certificate}, not merely a
sufficiency statement: for finite real joint mass $J(\theta,x)$, a deterministic
rule is Bayes-optimal precisely when it is \emph{pointwise} MAP, returning a
joint-mass maximizer at every sample. A rule that is not pointwise MAP strictly
loses prior-averaged success; on a tie two distinct rules can both be optimal, so
optimality does not by itself force a unique answer, while at a sample with a
\emph{strict} maximizer the optimal choice is forced and unique. For nonnegative
joint mass and positive predictive probability, the conditional posterior
equals $1$ at a candidate exactly when every candidate of positive joint mass
equals it; the positive-mass hypothesis is essential, and zero-mass samples are
handled separately.
\end{remark}


\paragraph{Rotation classes and the choice of prior.}
The prior is a prior on \emph{equivalence classes}, not on literal spellings.
A uniform prior over circular words would instead weight each class in
proportion to its orbit size, which is $n$ for a primitive length-$n$ word but
smaller for a word with a shorter period; that asymmetry is an artifact of the
representation, not of the data (Remark~\ref{rem:cyclic}). We therefore take the
uniform prior over rotation classes, the symmetric choice under which ML is the
prior-average optimum. That MAP and ML coincide for the exact iid read
likelihood under the uniform prior on rotation classes is kernel-checked
(\texttt{AssemblyP1.GenomeOrbitAdapter.uniform\_orbit\_prior\_ML}); the
circular oriented read model and the uniform start sampling are external
modeling inputs. This is a modeling choice, not a theorem: with a
nonuniform prior or a different loss the prior-average optimum is MAP for that
prior and loss, and the ML rule is no longer claimed optimal. The theorem says
nothing about a \emph{fixed} truth, where the relevant question is instead
identifiability (Section~\ref{sec:population}) and its finite-sample
approximation (Section~\ref{sec:finite}).

\begin{remark}[Infallibility on a truth-dependent event is not Bayes optimality]
\label{rem:map-vs-infallible}
Let $E=\{(S,a),(D,b)\}$ be a truth-dependent event and let a rule $\delta_E$ be
\emph{correct by construction} on $E$ ($a\mapsto S$, $b\mapsto D$). With a
uniform prior and $P_S(a)=\tfrac14$, $P_S(b)=\tfrac34$, $P_D(a)=\tfrac34$,
$P_D(b)=\tfrac14$, the rule $\delta_E$ has prior-averaged success
$\tfrac14$, whereas the unconstrained MAP rule --- which returns $D$ on $a$ and
$S$ on $b$ --- succeeds with probability $\tfrac34$. Being infallible on $E$ is
a conditional guarantee, not a prior-average one, and an $R$-only rule need not
know that $E=F(S,R)$. This is precisely how the finite counterexamples coexist
with Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal}. The two accuracies $\tfrac14$ and
$\tfrac34$, and the strict inequality between them, are kernel-checked in
\texttt{AssemblyP1.FiniteMapOptimality}
(\texttt{accuracy\_deltaE}, \texttt{accuracy\_deltaMap},
\texttt{map\_beats\_infallible\_on\_E}).
\end{remark}

The theorem concerns the \emph{prior average}, not optimality for every
fixed truth. It applies to the true joint likelihood, not automatically to
the independent-binomial surrogate of Definition~\ref{def:likelihoods}.
A uniform positive prior is sufficient for ML and MAP to coincide, but not
necessary: on a restricted observation family the candidate-dependent
likelihood ratios may never cross prior thresholds, so nonuniform priors can
share the ML argmax on every observable sample.
Additional independent observations cannot reduce the \emph{optimal
prior-average} reconstruction probability, because the decision rule can
ignore them \parencite{berger1985}. Per-truth success can nevertheless oscillate,
as Proposition~\ref{prop:nonmonotone} demonstrates.



The finite-sample recovery and one-sided large-deviation calculations
are developed in Appendix~\ref{app:decision-bounds}. They sharpen
bounds without changing the central distinction between a
truth-dependent reconstruction certificate and unrestricted ML.

\subsection{A worked example, and five claims that must not be conflated}
\label{sec:five-claims}

The statements above are logically independent, and a small example makes the
independence vivid before the assembly-specific instances.

\begin{example}[Prior-average optimality without pointwise success]
\label{ex:worked-bayes}
Let $\Theta=\{S,D\}$ with uniform prior $\pi(S)=\pi(D)=\tfrac12$, and let a
single observed read type take values in $\{a,b\}$ with $P_S(a)=\tfrac34$ and
$P_D(a)=\tfrac12$. Then $\pi(S)P_S(a)=\tfrac38$ and
$\pi(D)P_D(a)=\tfrac14$, so MAP --- and, by uniformity of the prior, ML ---
returns $S$ on $a$ and $D$ on $b$. Its prior-averaged success is
$\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac58$, which is optimal
(Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal}). Yet when the truth is $S$ and the
observation is $b$ --- an event of probability $\tfrac14$ --- the rule returns
$D$ and is wrong. Prior-average optimality therefore coexists with a
positive-probability pointwise failure. This is an elementary direct
computation, not a new theorem.
\end{example}

The assembly analogues of the two directions are: Example~\ref{ex:ABAB}
realizes the stronger failure in which two candidates have \emph{identical}
read laws, so no rule can beat chance (a failure of identifiability), whereas
the historical witnesses $W_1,W_2$ realize strict pointwise failure while the
population laws remain identifiable. The five notions are collected below with
their exact quantifiers, hypotheses, and evidence.

\subsection{Finite and population conclusions}

\begin{counterexample}[Two historical same-length likelihood reversals]
\label{ex:W1W2}
Under the historical $I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ predicate and the source's
reverse-complement molecule read types, $S=AAATAT$ loses to $D=AAAAAT$
at $L=3$ under the exact and fixed-$N$ binomial objectives (ratios $3$
and $5$), while $S=ATATACAC$ loses to $D=ATACACAC$
(ratios $3/2$ and $9/5$). All are same-length MB09 flows, and
the latter witness also satisfies per-occurrence feasibility. These are
strict finite-sample statements, not failures of population identifiability.
The certificates and exact observations are in
Theorems~\ref{thm:cex-historical-W1}--\ref{thm:cex-historical-W2}.
\end{counterexample}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional same-length structural rigidity]
\label{thm:open-rigidity}
Under the project's placement-based $\Is$, oriented read types, equal
candidate length, and genuine MB09 candidacy of the truth and
competitor, every candidate has the truth's complete spectrum and is
a cyclic rotation of it. Every spectrum-based score therefore ties.
\end{theorem}
This is a rigidity and identifiability theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:rigidity}, Corollary~\ref{cor:same-length-rotation}),
not a likelihood-specific inequality. Truth candidacy is necessary:
$AABB\to ABAB$ is a same-length strict counterexample without it.
Equal length is independently necessary: $AAATT\to AAAATT$ is a
variable-length counterexample even with full observed support
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-variable-length}).

\begin{theorem}[Population uniqueness]
\label{thm:open-population}
The true genome maximizes population expected log-likelihood over
every class containing it. Within the primitive-P2 oriented class
the maximizer is unique up to rotation
(Lemma~\ref{lem:gibbs}, Theorem~\ref{thm:population}).
\end{theorem}

The population theorem does not assert that every finite sample favours
the truth. Conversely, the finite counterexamples do not contradict
Bayes-optimality or asymptotic recovery on the identifiable primitive-P2
class. The modeling and source-referent distinctions are documented in
Appendices~\ref{app:modeling} and~\ref{app:referents}.

\subsection{Structural identifiability landmarks}
\label{sec:structural-landmarks}

Two structural results sharpen the identifiability landscape beyond the
primitive-P2 population theorem. The first is kernel-checked in full; the
second has a kernel-checked divisor-sum core and a paper-level count whose
BEST premise is external. Both are conditional on the oriented
complete-spectrum model and do not by themselves settle which candidate
universe the 2016 sources intended.

\begin{theorem}[Variable-length primitive identifiability iff nonbranching]
\label{thm:identifiable-iff-nonbranching}
Let $S$ be a primitive circular genome of length $G$ and read length $L>1$.
Then $S$ is identifiable among primitive candidates of arbitrary length from
its normalized complete $L$-spectrum if and only if its spectrum-support
graph has no vertex with two distinct outgoing edge types.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A primitive genome spells an Eulerian circuit in its read-support
multigraph. If every support vertex has one outgoing edge type,
strong connectivity forces a directed cycle; every primitive
genome having that normalized spectrum traverses that cycle once
and is the same word up to rotation. Conversely, on branching
support, the primitive-spelling construction for a balanced
multigraph produces a primitive Eulerian spelling of twice
the truth's edge multiplicities. It has twice the truth's length,
the same \emph{normalized} spectrum, and cannot be a rotation
of the original genome. This direction uses the nontrivial
primitive-spelling lemma, proved in
\texttt{AssemblyP1.ScalarPrimitiveSpellings}, not merely balance
of the circulation.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Same-length complete-spectrum fibre count and singleton criterion]
\label{thm:fibre-count}
Let $S$ be a circular genome of length $G$ and read length $L\ge2$, and let
$c=\spec_L(S)$ be its complete $L$-spectrum viewed as a balanced capacity.
Write $g=\gcd\{c(e):0<c(e)\}$. The number of same-length rotation classes
with spectrum $c$ is
\[
  N(c)=\sum_{h\mid g}P_h,\qquad
  P_h=\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}\,B_{h/d},
\]
where $c_0=c/g$, $c_h=h c_0$,
$d_u(h)=\sum_{e:\operatorname{tail}(e)=u}c_h(e)$,
and $\tau_r(c_h)$ is the weighted directed in-arborescence count
rooted at any support vertex $r$. The quantity
$B_h=\tau_r(c_h)\prod_u(d_u(h)-1)!/\prod_e c_h(e)!$
does \emph{not} depend on $r$ (by the balanced directed
Matrix--Tree/BEST identity). The function $\mu$ is the M\"obius
function. Equivalently $N(c)=\sum_{k\mid g}\varphi(g/k)/(g/k)\cdot B_k$.
The fibre is a singleton modulo rotation iff the support is nonbranching,
or it branches with $g=1$ and $B_1=1$; if it branches and $g>1$ then
$N(c)\ge2$. The independent arithmetic verifier and expanded convention audit
are linked from Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Temporarily label the $c_h(e)$ indistinguishable copies of each
edge type. By the directed BEST theorem, the number of linear,
labelled Euler tours beginning at vertex $r$ is
$d_r(h)\,\tau_r(c_h)\prod_u(d_u(h)-1)!$.
An unlabelled cyclic spelling with rotational stabilizer size
$s$ has $d_r(h)/s$ distinct linearizations starting at $r$,
each with $\prod_e c_h(e)!$ labellings. Cancelling these
factors shows
\[
 B_h=\sum_{[W]\,:\,\spec(W)=c_h}\frac{1}{s(W)}.
\]
The root independence of $\tau_r(c_h)$ follows because the
balanced graph's Laplacian has both row and column sums zero:
its principal cofactors, which count in-arborescences, coincide.
Self-loops enter the out-degree factorials but not an
arborescence.

Every cyclic spelling is a power of a unique primitive cyclic
spelling. If $P_j$ counts primitive orbits at level $j\mid h$,
then $B_h=\sum_{j\mid h}(j/h)P_j$. M\"obius inversion gives
the displayed $P_h$, and summing over $h\mid g$ yields $N(c)$.
Reordering the divisor sums and using
$\sum_{d\mid m}\mu(d)/d=\varphi(m)/m$ gives the totient form.

On nonbranching support every spelling follows a single directed
cycle, so the fibre has one orbit. If the support branches and
$g=1$, then $N(c)=B_1$ gives the criterion.
If it branches and $g>1$, take the repeated spelling $W_0^g$
of $g c_0$ and the primitive spelling of the same capacity
supplied by the branching primitive-spelling construction.
One is a proper power and the other is primitive, so they are
not rotations; hence $N(c)\ge2$.
The underlying weighted BEST theorem is classical; the
divisor identities and primitive-spelling lemmas are
separately kernel-checked as stated in the proof index.

The transition-count enumeration is closely related to
Whittle's Markov-chain formula
\parencite{whittle1955} and to the
Euler-tour shuffling of sequences preserving $k$-let
frequencies \parencite{kandel1996}.
The Möbius/totient correction here counts primitive
\emph{circular rotation classes}; the cited results
are related antecedents, not citations for this
specific primitive-orbit formula.
\end{proof}
