\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{From a genome to a collection of reads}

A genome is a sequence of letters from the DNA alphabet $\{A,C,G,T\}$.
Sequencing does not normally reveal that sequence all at once. Instead, it
produces many shorter strings, called \emph{reads}, whose original positions
are unknown. Assembly asks how to recover the sequence from those pieces.

We study a deliberately simple model. The genome is circular, so a read can
wrap around its end. Each read has the same length $L$ and starts at a
uniformly chosen position; different starts are sampled independently, and
the letters are read without errors. Unless specified otherwise, we keep
track of which direction each read was read in. These assumptions isolate
the mathematical question; they are not a model of every complication of
real sequencing.
For example, the circular genome $\texttt{ACGTA}$ has five possible
length-three reads:
\[
  \texttt{ACG},\quad\texttt{CGT},\quad\texttt{GTA},\quad
  \texttt{TAA},\quad\texttt{AAC}.
\]
The last two wrap around the end of the written sequence. A sequencing
experiment could see each of these once, or see some several times and miss
others entirely. Its output is a \emph{multiset} of reads, not their original
order around the genome. Since no position is distinguished on a circle,
we consider two genomes identical if one is a cyclic rotation of the other.

Two questions arise from the same data. \emph{Reconstruction} asks which
sequence the pieces fit together to form. \emph{Maximum likelihood} asks
which candidate sequence would have been most likely to generate the
particular collection of pieces we happened to observe. These are related,
but they are not the same question.

\subsection{Why overlaps sometimes reveal the genome}

When two reads share a long suffix and prefix, that overlap suggests they
might come from neighboring positions. Trouble arises when a stretch of
letters appears in several places. If a genome contains both
$\texttt{\ldots CAAG\ldots}$ and $\texttt{\ldots TAAC\ldots}$,
a read $\texttt{AA}$ does not say which copy it came from; even
$\texttt{CAA}$ tells us only what lies on one side. A read
$\texttt{CAAG}$ extends beyond the first $\texttt{AA}$ on \emph{both}
sides, distinguishing its surrounding context. We say that it
\emph{bridges} that repeat copy. In particular, a length-two repeat
requires a read of length at least four to be bridged on both sides.

Without enough such context, the difficulty is not merely that a
particular algorithm is weak. Different genomes can produce exactly
the same reads with exactly the same probabilities.

\begin{example}[Two primitive genomes with the same read law]
\label{ex:ABAB}
Consider the circular DNA genomes
\[
  S=\texttt{AACAG},\qquad D=\texttt{AAGAC},
\]
with read length $L=2$. Neither genome is a rotation of the other and
both are primitive (their length is prime and neither is constant).
Both have precisely the five possible reads
$\texttt{AA},\texttt{AC},\texttt{CA},\texttt{AG},\texttt{GA}$,
each with probability $1/5$. Thus even infinitely many independent
length-two reads cannot distinguish the two. Structural ambiguity
does \emph{not} require a periodic competitor.
\end{example}

\subsection{From reads to a graph algorithm}
\label{sec:intro:assembly-operations}

Overlaps turn reconstruction into a graph problem. Return to
$\texttt{ACGTA}$ and its length-three reads. In a simple
\emph{de Bruijn graph}, each read is an edge joining its first two
letters to its last two:
\[
\mathrm{AC}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{ACG}}\mathrm{CG}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CGT}}\mathrm{GT}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{GTA}}\mathrm{TA}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{TAA}}\mathrm{AA}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{AAC}}\mathrm{AC}.
\]
Following the edges around this cycle spells the original circular
genome, up to rotation. With repeats the graph may branch, and
different ways of following its edges can spell different genomes.
This is why a guarantee about repeats is a useful guarantee about
reconstruction.

\textcite{shomorony2016} developed an efficient
\emph{Not-So-Greedy} assembly algorithm using overlaps between observed
reads. Its graph is different from the teaching example above. Their
theorem says, roughly, that sufficient read coverage together with
bridging of certain repeated regions allows that graph to recover the
true genome uniquely up to rotation. The precise conditions concern
triple repeats and pairs of interleaved repeats, rather than every
repeat, and are given in Section~\ref{sec:open}. This is a guarantee
about reconstructing a sequence from the read strings.

\subsection{Which genome makes the sample most probable?}

The number of times a read occurs in a candidate genome matters to
likelihood. If a candidate $D$ has length $|D|$, and a length-$L$
read $w$ occurs at $c$ of its positions, a uniformly sampled read
equals $w$ with probability $c/|D|$. For independent reads we multiply
those probabilities. Uniform \emph{start positions} therefore do not
mean uniform \emph{read strings}.

Here is a minimal example. Let $S=\texttt{AAC}$ be the true circular
genome and $D=\texttt{AAAC}$ a competing genome, with $L=2$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Read & Probability under $S$ & Probability under $D$\\
\midrule
$\texttt{AA}$ & $1/3$ & $1/2$\\
$\texttt{AC}$ & $1/3$ & $1/4$\\
$\texttt{CA}$ & $1/3$ & $1/4$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

One observed read $\texttt{AC}$ favors the truth: $1/3>1/4$.
But two reads $\texttt{AA},\texttt{AA}$ favor the competitor:
$(1/2)^2=1/4$ against $(1/3)^2=1/9$. Both observations are
possible under $S$. A sample need not have the same proportions
as the genome's full collection of possible reads.

This small example illustrates sampling fluctuations, \emph{not} a
reconstruction theorem: we have not claimed that those one or two reads
determine $S$. Its exact arithmetic is reproduced by the verifier
listed in Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}. Next we show that
the mismatch can persist even with much stronger structural evidence.

\subsection{Bridging can hold when likelihood prefers another genome}
\label{sec:intro:two-questions}

\textcite[Discussion]{shomorony2016} asked whether their
conditions guaranteeing reconstruction also guarantee that the
maximum-likelihood genome is the true one. The question is striking
because reconstructing a genome sounds as though it should make that
genome the best explanation of the reads. But reconstruction uses
their \emph{overlaps}, while likelihood also depends on \emph{how
often} each kind of read was sampled.

Consider the circular truth $S=\texttt{AAATT}$, with read length
$L=3$. Suppose all five of its circular windows are observed,
together with one extra occurrence of $\texttt{AAA}$:
\[
  r=(\texttt{AAA},\texttt{AAA},\texttt{AAT},
     \texttt{ATT},\texttt{TTA},\texttt{TAA}).
\]
The reads include \emph{every} length-three substring of the truth.
In particular, they satisfy the original matched-read-string coverage
condition: a read matches at every genome start. The only triple
repeat has three single-letter $\texttt{A}$ copies; these are
straddled respectively by $\texttt{TAA}$, $\texttt{AAA}$ and
$\texttt{AAT}$. No interleaved pair violates the historical bridging
clause. Thus the historical reconstruction hypothesis
$I_s^{\mathrm{match}}$ holds, not merely our weaker
sampled-placement predicate. The overlap reconstruction guarantee
identifies the truth up to rotation.

Nevertheless, the longer genome $D=\texttt{AAAATT}$ is more likely to
produce the six reads. Every read type occurs once per turn of $S$,
but $\texttt{AAA}$ occurs twice per turn of $D$ while the other
four types occur once. Under the \emph{exact} independently sampled,
uniform-start model,
\[
  \frac{\Pr(r\mid D)}{\Pr(r\mid S)}
   = \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^6\,2^2
   =\frac{15625}{11664}>1.
\]
The multinomial coefficient for unordered reads cancels from this
ratio. The same effect holds for an infinite family of read samples
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-variable-length}).

A more fundamental issue is \emph{existence}: for
$S=\texttt{AAACAC}$ and seven carefully specified reads,
Theorem~\ref{thm:unattained-ML} proves that no circular
genome at all maximizes unrestricted exact likelihood,
even though finite genomes approach its supremum.
The general criterion (Theorem~ef{thm:flow-supremum-attainment})
is exact: the likelihood has a finite optimizing genome if
and only if some maximum-likelihood balanced read law is
rational and supported on a connected overlap graph.
A rational \emph{disconnected} optimum can also cause
non-attainment (Example~ef{ex:unattained-disconnected}).
Thus a length restriction or a prior is needed before
unrestricted scoring always defines an estimator.

In fact, for any fixed genome whose read-support graph branches,
Theorem~\ref{thm:unrestricted-branching-rate} shows that the
probability of being an exact-ML maximizer over \emph{all}
candidate lengths is at most $O(n^{-(E-V)/2})$, where $E$ and
$V$ count distinct read edges and overlap vertices. This is a
fixed-genome, unrestricted-length result; it does not contradict
positive ML recovery on the restricted primitive-P2 class.

There is no contradiction with the positive same-length theorem below:
that theorem only compares candidates of the \emph{same} genome length
and with the \emph{same complete read support}. Here $|D|=6$ and
$|S|=5$. Same-length failures under the historical matched-string
condition also exist, but require distinguishing reverse-complement
molecule observations (Theorems~\ref{thm:cex-historical-W1} and
\ref{thm:cex-historical-W2}).

One could first discard all candidate genomes failing a
reconstruction condition and then maximize likelihood only among
those left. But that is a \emph{different optimization problem}
from finding the most likely genome among all permitted candidates.
Section~\ref{sec:two-questions} makes that distinction precise,
including what happens when an additional promise about the truth
is included in a Bayesian model.

\subsection{What this paper establishes}

The central lesson is that there are two obstacles to inferring the
true genome: \emph{ambiguity in how reads fit together}, and
\emph{random variation in the observed read counts}. More
sequencing can reduce the second obstacle, but cannot resolve two
genomes that produce exactly the same read distribution.

Our assembly-specific contributions include circulation rigidity under
bridging (Theorem~\ref{thm:rigidity}), the primitive-genome
identifiability boundary (Theorem~\ref{thm:identifiable-iff-nonbranching}),
an exact same-length spectrum-fibre count (Theorem~\ref{thm:fibre-count};
the full count is proved at paper level), the primitive-P2 spectrum
normalization lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:scaling}), and the
primitive-P2 genome-length bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:p2-finite-class}).
Our finite counterexamples and source-model distinctions also
contribute to this picture. By contrast, MAP's average-case optimality,
the Gibbs inequality, Hellinger/Chernoff bounds, and the strong
law are standard statistical tools that we apply with stated hypotheses,
not new statistical principles.

\paragraph{A positive structural result.}
In a restricted model with oriented reads, equal-length candidates,
and explicit requirements on which reads the candidates contain,
bridging makes the true genome's complete read counts rigid:
competitors cannot rearrange those counts to improve likelihood.
Under these assumptions, the true genome is the unique
maximum-likelihood candidate up to rotation
(Theorem~\ref{thm:rigidity} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:same-length-rotation}).
Those extra assumptions matter; they are not supplied automatically
by reconstruction alone.

In plain terms: if every distinct length-$L$ read of $S$ was
observed, and $S$ has no triple repeat of length at least $L-1$,
then no same-length genome with the same complete read support can
change the length-$L$ read counts. Their likelihoods consequently
\emph{tie}; full repeat-bridging hypotheses are additionally used
to prove uniqueness of the circular spelling, up to rotation.

\paragraph{Finite-sample counterexamples.}
Over larger candidate classes, the wrong genome may receive a
higher likelihood even when the read set covers and bridges
the truth. We give exact examples for different sampling objectives,
including the original matched-read formulation
(Section~\ref{sec:finite}). Some failures occur even when the
competing genome has the same length as the truth. The example above
shows why: a competitor can assign more probability to a read type
that happened to be sampled.

\paragraph{What the full read distribution reveals.}
If we knew the probability of \emph{every} possible read exactly,
the truth would always maximize expected log-likelihood. The
remaining question would be whether another genome generates
the very same distribution, as in Example~\ref{ex:ABAB}.
We prove uniqueness when the truth \emph{and all competitors}
are primitive genomes satisfying the same repeat condition, called P2
(Theorem~\ref{thm:population}). Without that restriction on the
competitors, uniqueness among primitive genomes holds exactly
when the read-overlap support graph is nonbranching
(Theorem~\ref{thm:identifiable-iff-nonbranching}). For this class we also give an
exponentially decreasing upper bound on the probability of a
maximum-likelihood error as independent reads are collected
(Theorem~\ref{thm:p2-uniform-rate}). This is a theoretical
guarantee, not a claim that the explicit bound gives a
practical sequencing depth.

It does not establish how likelihood performs near the sequencing
threshold where coverage and bridging first become likely.
That high-probability threshold regime remains open (Question~8
in Section~\ref{sec:open-questions-final}).

A different positive result avoids a bridgeability requirement
on \emph{competitors}: for a primitive P2 truth of length $G$,
every circular genome of length below $2G$ with the same
oriented read distribution is a rotation of the truth
(Theorem~\ref{thm:one-sided-length-window}).
A known size bound strictly below $2G$ yields exponential
exact-ML recovery; an explicit geometric length prior
gives a well-defined, eventually correct MAP estimator
even without a finite length bound
(Proposition~\ref{prop:length-prior-map}).
The latter results are for fixed truths, not uniformly
growing genome sequences.

\paragraph{Why maximum likelihood is still worth studying.}
There is a general statistical reason to use it: if one assigns
equal prior probabilities to a finite collection of candidate
genomes (up to rotation), maximum likelihood has the highest
\emph{average} probability of identifying the truth among all
decision rules (Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal}). But being optimal
\emph{on average} does not mean always being correct for a
particular genome or sample. That difference is central here.

\paragraph{General structural criteria.}
We also prove criteria for when integral read-count flows are
rigid, and for score dominance under the
\emph{fixed-size product-binomial approximation}
(Theorems~\ref{thm:graver-rigidity} and
\ref{thm:binomial-weights}). These apply to stated graph and
likelihood models; they should not be confused with a universal
solution for every physical read-overlap graph.
