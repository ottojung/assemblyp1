\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Genomes, reads, and the repeat obstacle}

A genome is a long string over the four-letter DNA alphabet $\{A,C,G,T\}$;
many bacterial genomes are circular, and we model the genome as a circular word
so that every position has a well-defined neighborhood on both sides. A
sequencing experiment does not read the genome end to end. It samples many
short \emph{reads}: each read records the length-$L$ window at a start position
chosen uniformly at random along the genome, independently of the other reads.
The assembly problem is to infer the genome from the observed reads.

The obstacle to assembly is repetition. When the same substring occurs twice
in the genome, reads drawn from the two copies are identical, and the data do
not record which copy produced them. The ambiguity is structural: two genomes
with the same length-$L$ window spectrum induce exactly the same read
distribution, so no scoring of the reads can separate them.

\begin{example}[Structural ambiguity before the repeat boundary]
\label{ex:ABAB}
Let the alphabet be $\{A,B\}$ and let $L=2$. The circular genomes
\[
  S = AAABAB, \qquad D = AABAAB
\]
of length $6$ are not cyclic rotations of one another, yet their circular
$2$-mer spectra agree:
\[
  \spec_2(S) = \spec_2(D) = \{\,AA:2,\ AB:2,\ BA:2\,\}.
\]
Indeed $S$ has windows $AA,AA,AB,BA,AB,BA$ and $D$ has windows
$AA,AB,BA,AA,AB,BA$. Under uniform-start error-free sampling the observed read
distribution is the same for both genomes, so \emph{no} likelihood objective
--- exact, approximate, or flow-based --- can prefer $S$ to $D$ here.
\statusline{\srcfact{} for the spectra and the rotation claim;
\mathfact{} for the resulting tie.}
\end{example}

\subsection{Bridging: the structural evidence inside overlapping reads}

A read \emph{bridges} a copy of a repeat when it extends strictly beyond the
copy on both sides. The read then ties that copy to its unique flanking
context: the copy is no longer interchangeable with the other copy, because
only one placement makes the read fit. Bridging is the local, structural
evidence with which overlapping reads resolve repeats.

Shomorony, Kim, Courtade, and Tse \cite{shomorony2016} made this precise. They
gave repeat-bridging conditions on the \emph{true} genome --- every position
covered by some read, every triple repeat bridged at all copies, and every
interleaved repeat pair bridged --- under which the reads determine the true
circular genome uniquely up to cyclic shift. This is an
\emph{information-feasibility} guarantee: a statement about what the reads
contain, proved through the graph structure of the reads. Their
Not-So-Greedy construction is a \emph{structural reconstruction} method: it
does not score candidates but builds the overlap graph of the observed reads
and reads off its Eulerian cycle, and the bridging conditions are a
\emph{sufficient} theorem for that procedure to spell the truth exactly
(Section~\ref{sec:open}). Many genomes can generate the same reads ---
Example~\ref{ex:ABAB} gives two with identical read distributions --- so
read-generation consistency is a much weaker property than bridging
feasibility; within the full fixed source assumptions, at most one genome up
to cyclic rotation satisfies the strong guarantee for the same observations.

\subsection{Maximum likelihood as the simplest principled statistical benchmark}

The same randomness that makes assembly hard also supplies its most natural
benchmark. A candidate genome is not merely a string; through the sampling
process it induces a probability distribution over datasets. The
\emph{maximum-likelihood} (ML) rule scores each candidate by the probability it
assigns to the observed reads and outputs the highest scorer. As a benchmark
the ML rule is exceptionally clean: it has no tuning parameters, no heuristic
steps, and no candidate-class restrictions built in, and under a uniform prior
over genome equivalence classes with zero-one loss it is Bayes-optimal
\emph{on average} --- it maximizes the prior-averaged chance of correct
classification at every sample size, a fact we state precisely and
kernel-check in its finite form in Section~\ref{sec:open}. It therefore needs
no defense as a default question: asking whether the true genome \emph{is}
the ML genome is the conceptually simplest principled statistical question one
can ask about assembly. The interest lies entirely in the answer, and the
answer, we will see, is subtle.

Two features of the benchmark matter throughout. First, the reads are
generated by sampling \emph{start positions uniformly}, not read types: a
candidate assigns to each distinct read string a probability proportional to
its multiplicity in the candidate, so the likelihood is sensitive to
repetition, not just to content (Section~\ref{sec:model}). Second, Bayes
optimality is a statement \emph{averaged over a prior}: at any fixed genome
the rule can err, and our finite counterexamples show it erring strictly. The
benchmark is optimal on average, not infallible pointwise.

\subsection{The 2016 question: natural, nontrivial, and twofold}

A different tradition \cite{medvedev2009} scores candidates instead of
reconstructing; the \emph{likelihood} of a candidate genome is the probability
that uniform sampling from it would produce the observed reads, and the
\emph{maximum-likelihood genome} is the candidate with the highest score.
Likelihood is an objective function on candidate genomes, not a procedure for
reconstructing one; everything in this paper is a statement about such
rankings.

Shomorony et al.\ close their Discussion with a question that sits between the
two lines \cite[Discussion]{shomorony2016}:

\begin{quote}
``Understanding whether bridging conditions can be used to guarantee that the
maximum-likelihood sequence is the true sequence is currently an open
question.''
\end{quote}

The question is \emph{natural} because both sides are canonical: bridging
conditions are the literature's sufficient conditions for exact structural
reconstruction, and ML is the simplest principled statistical benchmark, so
the sentence asks whether a structural guarantee implies a statistical one.
It is \emph{nontrivial} because the two sides live in different experiments.
The structural side certifies that the \emph{truth} is feasible for the
observation; the statistical side ranks \emph{all} candidates by likelihood.
Conditioning the candidate set on feasibility --- knowing the truth satisfies
the bridging predicate --- changes the experiment, and an algorithm that sees
only the reads need not know that conditioning event. Whether the implication
holds therefore depends on choices the published sentence does not make: the
objective, the read-type convention, the candidate class, and whether the
conclusion asserts that the truth is \emph{a} maximizer or the \emph{unique}
one. We separate those choices explicitly rather than absorbing them into
notation, and we show in Section~\ref{sec:open} that the question splits into
a conditional part that is a corollary of the reconstruction theorem and an
unconditional part that is the genuinely nontrivial published comparison.

\subsection{Contributions}

This manuscript reframes the 2016 question as a question about a statistical
decision rule, and proves positive and negative results about when the true
genome is a likelihood maximizer.

\paragraph{The statistical frame, made precise.}
We introduce the random-read generation model and the exact candidate
likelihood, with uniform start sampling and the resulting nonuniform read-type
probabilities (Section~\ref{sec:model}). We then state Bayes/MAP optimality
as a theorem with its exact scope --- fixed finite candidate universe,
correctly specified common observation model, uniform prior over equivalence
classes, zero-one loss; prior-averaged optimality at every finite $n$; and
explicit failure of every strengthening (per-truth optimality, arbitrary
prior or loss, the MB09 binomial pseudo-likelihood as exact Bayes ML). The
theorem itself is textbook \claimT; a finite exact evaluator for it exists as
an issue~\#267 worktree draft that does not yet compile against the pinned
toolchain, and the canonical kernel endpoint --- including the genome-scale
adapter --- is owned by issue~\#269 and in progress. We therefore label the
theorem \claimT{} and mark the kernel composition explicitly as pending,
rather than writing ``Lean-proved'' for a conditional derivation.

\paragraph{The two-question distinction.}
We separate the question the published sentence asks from the question a
reconstruction corollary answers. If the candidate set is constrained to the
feasible fiber --- the candidates themselves satisfying the bridging predicate
--- then, knowing the truth is feasible, the conditional posterior is a
singleton and any valid conditional MAP returns the truth; this is largely a
corollary of the 2016 reconstruction theorem, not a new unconditional ML
result. If instead the original unrestricted likelihood is maximized over its
original candidate universe, assuming only that the truth is feasible, the
implication is not automatic, and this is the genuinely nontrivial published
comparison. We show that the extra conditioning is truth-dependent --- it
changes the experiment --- and that an algorithm seeing only the reads need
not know the conditioning event, with a kernel-checked $2\times2$ witness.
\claimP{} for the distinction; \claimK{} for the witness.

\paragraph{Positive: circulation rigidity.}
Under the bridging condition of Definition~\ref{def:Is}, the truth's length-$L$
spectrum is the unique positive circulation of its window-support graph
(Theorem~\ref{cor:same-length-zero}): no other assignment of positive
multiplicities to the observed windows can be balanced and sum to the genome
length. Consequently, on the same-length oriented slice, every spelled
candidate ties the truth under any spectrum-based objective, and no strict
same-length counterexample exists for \emph{any} genome length, read length,
or alphabet. With the complete-spectrum uniqueness step, every such candidate
is in fact a cyclic rotation of the truth (Corollary~\ref{rem:same-length-rotation}).
The extra assumptions this theorem needs --- oriented reads, equal candidate
length, the full-overlap support restriction, and truth candidature --- are
project-level restrictions, honestly labelled in Section~\ref{sec:finite};
they do not follow from the published sentence.

\paragraph{Negative: finite-sample reweighting, not a paradox.}
The equal-length restriction is essential. We exhibit finite observations in
which the truth satisfies the bridging conditions and a competitor still has
strictly greater likelihood, under the exact multinomial over all circular
candidates (Theorem~\ref{rem:cex-exact}), under its fixed-length binomial
approximation (Theorem~\ref{rem:cex-binomial}), and under the
reverse-complement molecule reading of the Section~6.2 flow
(Theorem~\ref{rem:cex-molecule}). The mechanism is empirical read reweighting:
the exact objective scores the \emph{observed} read-type frequencies, and a
competitor can improve its score by reallocating multiplicity toward
over-represented observed types. Allowing a single extra symbol reintroduces
a strict witness (Theorem~\ref{rem:cex-variable-length}). The same failure
survives under the \emph{source's own} read-string predicate of supplement
\S6.4, where coverage and bridging quantify observed read strings matched at
any genomic position: two same-length molecule witnesses
(Theorem~\ref{rem:cex-historical-W1}
and~\ref{rem:cex-historical-W2}) satisfy historical coverage and
matched-string bridging at full strength while beating the truth under both
source-named objectives. Each witness's scope --- observation model, candidate
membership, historical read-string versus placement-based coverage, candidate
lengths, prior family, and the exact likelihood formula --- is preserved
explicitly, and each is a pointwise finite-sample failure consistent with,
not contradictory to, prior-average Bayes optimality.

\paragraph{Population uniqueness.}
We then study maximum likelihood when the read distribution is known exactly,
without sampling fluctuations. In this setting the true genome always
maximizes likelihood --- a Gibbs/KL identity (Lemma~\ref{rem:gibbs})
--- so uniqueness is the only question. Uniqueness is nontrivial: it reduces to
spectrum identifiability, and for primitive genomes satisfying a structural
repeat condition (P2) it is proved in full, with no external premise
(Theorem~\ref{rem:population}). The complete-spectrum uniqueness
step that this theorem needs is the deep combinatorial ingredient; the
maximizer statement itself is routine. The population maximality holds for
\emph{any} candidate class containing the truth, however large; the
finite-candidate-space asymptotic recovery theorem is a different, external
statement, and neither is replaced by conditioning on information
feasibility.

\subsection{How to read this paper}

Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the sequencing and bridging model, the
random-read generation process, the exact candidate likelihood, and the
objectives. Section~\ref{sec:open} states the mathematical question precisely:
the two-question distinction, the Bayes-optimality theorem and its scope, and
the finite and population answers. Section~\ref{sec:finite} reports the
finite-sample results: the positive rigidity theorem, the finite
counterexamples with their exact scopes, and the source-predicate witnesses.
Section~\ref{sec:population} introduces the population model, the
KL/Gibbs maximality for any candidate class, and its uniqueness theorem.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws the failure modes and the five
distinguishable notions together and lists what remains open.

Appendices~\ref{app:modeling} and~\ref{app:ledger} explain the modeling
choices and consolidate the status of every claim under the five-label
proof-to-claim policy;
Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction} records how to rebuild the paper and re-run
every computational certificate; Appendix~\ref{app:referents} records the
source-referent audit behind Section~\ref{sec:open}. A reader who wants only
the mathematical story can read Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:discussion}
and treat the appendices as reference.
