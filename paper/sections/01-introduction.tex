\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Genomes, reads, and the repeat obstacle}

A genome is a long string over the four-letter DNA alphabet $\{A,C,G,T\}$;
many bacterial genomes are circular, and we model the genome as a circular word
so that every position has a well-defined neighborhood on both sides. A
sequencing experiment does not read the genome end to end. It samples many
short \emph{reads}: each read records the length-$L$ window at a start position
chosen uniformly at random along the genome, independently of the other reads.
The assembly problem is to infer the genome from the observed reads.

The obstacle to assembly is repetition. When the same substring occurs twice
in the genome, reads drawn from the two copies are identical, and the data do
not record which copy produced them. The ambiguity is structural: two genomes
with the same length-$L$ window spectrum induce exactly the same read
distribution, so no scoring of the reads can separate them.

\begin{example}[Structural ambiguity before the repeat boundary]
\label{ex:ABAB}
Let the alphabet be $\{A,B\}$ and let $L=2$. The circular genomes
\[
  S = AAABAB, \qquad D = AABAAB
\]
of length $6$ are not cyclic rotations of one another, yet their circular
$2$-mer spectra agree:
\[
  \spec_2(S) = \spec_2(D) = \{\,AA:2,\ AB:2,\ BA:2\,\}.
\]
Indeed $S$ has windows $AA,AA,AB,BA,AB,BA$ and $D$ has windows
$AA,AB,BA,AA,AB,BA$. Under uniform-start error-free sampling the observed read
distribution is the same for both genomes, so \emph{no} likelihood objective
--- exact, approximate, or flow-based --- can prefer $S$ to $D$ here.
\statusline{\computed{} for the explicit spectra and nonrotation;
\mathfact{} for the resulting equal read distributions.}
\end{example}

\subsection{From reads to a graph algorithm}
\label{sec:intro:assembly-operations}

The reconstruction step can be performed explicitly. Fix $L=3$ and consider
the circular genome $\texttt{ACGTA}$. Its five circular windows are
$\texttt{ACG}$, $\texttt{CGT}$, $\texttt{GTA}$, $\texttt{TAA}$, and
$\texttt{AAC}$. In the associated \emph{de Bruijn graph}, an $L$-mer
is an edge from its length-$(L-1)$ prefix to its length-$(L-1)$ suffix:
\[
\mathrm{AC}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{ACG}}\mathrm{CG}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CGT}}\mathrm{GT}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{GTA}}\mathrm{TA}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{TAA}}\mathrm{AA}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{AAC}}\mathrm{AC}.
\]
An Eulerian traversal uses each edge with its multiplicity and spells
$\texttt{ACGTA}$, up to rotation. Thus a family of reads becomes a finite
graph and assembly becomes a question about compatible cyclic traversals.
At repeated substrings, a vertex can branch and multiple traversals may
spell different genomes.

Shomorony et al.\ \cite{shomorony2016} use a distinct \emph{read-overlap
graph}: vertices are observed reads, and weighted edges represent
suffix-prefix overlaps. Their Not-So-Greedy construction retains the greedy
successor and an additional genuinely disagreeing extension, constructs
analogous predecessor edges, prunes spurious edges using effective overlaps,
and modifies edge multiplicities to obtain an Eulerian multigraph. Under
the coverage and repeat-bridging conditions described next, that multigraph
has a unique Eulerian cycle spelling the truth. The construction takes
$O(NL)$ time for $N$ reads of length $L$. The de Bruijn graph above is a
teaching example, not the graph produced by Not-So-Greedy.

\subsection{Bridging: the structural evidence inside overlapping reads}

A read \emph{bridges} a copy of a repeat when it extends strictly beyond the
copy on both sides. The read then ties that copy to its unique flanking
context: the copy is no longer interchangeable with the other copy, because
only one placement makes the read fit. Bridging is the local, structural
evidence with which overlapping reads resolve repeats.

Shomorony, Kim, Courtade, and Tse \cite{shomorony2016} made this precise. They
gave repeat-bridging conditions on the \emph{true} genome --- every position
covered by some read, every triple repeat bridged at all copies, and every
interleaved repeat pair bridged --- under which the reads determine the true
circular genome uniquely up to cyclic shift. This is an
\emph{information-feasibility} guarantee: a statement about what the reads
contain, proved through the graph structure of the reads.

\subsection{The maximum-likelihood question}

A different tradition \cite{medvedev2009} scores candidates instead of
reconstructing: the \emph{likelihood} of a candidate genome is the probability
that uniform sampling from it would produce the observed reads, and the
\emph{maximum-likelihood genome} is the candidate with the highest score.
Likelihood assigns a score to candidate genomes; selecting a maximizer
turns that objective into a reconstruction rule. There is a compelling reason
to study this rule. With a finite candidate class, a correct read-generation
model, and a uniform prior on rotation classes, \emph{maximum likelihood has the
highest prior-averaged probability of returning the true genome among all
algorithms}, including randomized ones
(Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal}). For nonuniform priors the corresponding
optimal rule is maximum a posteriori (MAP). This standard Bayesian
decision-theoretic result provides the motivation and benchmark for the paper,
not its solution: the harder assembly question is what can be said about the
truth's likelihood rank for \emph{particular} reads and structural conditions.
In particular, the guarantee applies to the actual read distribution,
not automatically to an approximate likelihood or to an optimizer over
flows that fail to spell genomes.

Shomorony et al.\ close their Discussion with a question that sits between the
two lines \cite[Discussion]{shomorony2016}:

\begin{quote}
``Understanding whether bridging conditions can be used to guarantee that the
maximum-likelihood sequence is the true sequence is currently an open
question.''
\end{quote}

This manuscript investigates that question: when do constraints on
sequencing reads make the true genome a likelihood maximizer, and when can
another genome be more likely? The answer turns on how the structural evidence
of Section~\ref{sec:intro} meets the statistical evidence that likelihood
scores, and on choices the published sentence does not make --- the objective,
the read-type convention, the candidate class, and whether the conclusion
asserts that the truth is \emph{a} maximizer or the \emph{unique} one. We
separate those choices explicitly rather than absorbing them into notation.

\subsection{Reconstruction and likelihood ask different questions}
\label{sec:intro:two-questions}

The graph algorithm answers a question of \emph{information and computation}:
when reads provide sufficient context, can we reconstruct the true circular
word? Maximum likelihood instead asks which candidate $D$ assigns the
largest probability to the \emph{observed read-type counts}. A finite sample
can exhibit uneven frequencies even when its overlaps determine the source,
so a reconstruction theorem does not automatically rank the source highest.

These two statements cannot be identified, because ``the maximum-likelihood
sequence is the true sequence'' names \emph{two} inequivalent questions. Let
$F(g,R)$ be a feasibility predicate --- for the 2016 question, the source's
coverage-and-bridging condition evaluated on candidate $g$ against the observed
read set $R$. \emph{Question~A (conditional)} restricts the candidate set to the
feasible fiber $\{\,g : F(g,R)\,\}$ and assumes the truth is in it; the
reconstruction theorem makes the positive-weight feasible fiber
$\{g:\pi(g)P_g(R)>0,\ F(g,R)\}$ a singleton up to rotation,
so conditional MAP returns the truth. The bare predicate $F$ can admit a
genome incapable of generating the observed reads. This is
largely a corollary of the reconstruction theorem, not a new likelihood result.
\emph{Question~B (unconditional)} assumes only that the truth is feasible,
$F(S,R)$, and maximizes the \emph{original, unrestricted} likelihood over the
\emph{original} candidate universe. That maximizer need not be the truth, and it
is the genuinely nontrivial published comparison. The two differ in one place:
whether optimization is restricted to the feasible fiber, an event defined
through the hidden truth and therefore not knowable from the reads alone
(Section~\ref{sec:two-questions}).

We also study population likelihood, where the exact read distribution
replaces a particular finite sample. A Gibbs/KL identity then gives
maximality, leaving spectrum uniqueness as the interesting combinatorial
question. Bayesian inference supplied with an external promise about the
truth is a distinct conditional model; detailed interpretation and
source-referent questions belong in Appendices~\ref{app:modeling}
and~\ref{app:referents}.

\subsection{Contributions}

This manuscript proves positive and negative results about when the true
genome is a likelihood maximizer, and clarifies how those results relate to the
deterministic reconstruction guarantee of Shomorony et al.\ (2016). The
unifying thread is \emph{identifiability}: whether the read distribution
distinguishes a genome from its competitors, at the structural, population,
and finite-sample levels. The results below have different proof content and
deserve to be read on their own terms, rather than ranked by how closely they
resemble a general optimality slogan.

\paragraph{Motivation: optimal average reconstruction.}
The finite Bayes/MAP theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes-optimal}) establishes the
correctness-probability benchmark: MAP dominates every deterministic and
randomized reconstruction rule on prior-average zero-one success, and ML
coincides with MAP under a uniform prior. Its Lean formalization also proves
that optimal average success cannot decrease when genuinely additional
information is observed
(\kernelcheck{} \texttt{AssemblyP1.IidMapMonotonicity.iid\_map\_success\_monotone}). These are general decision-theoretic facts, not
novel assembly-specific identifiability results; they explain \emph{why}
the likelihood question is worth asking.

\paragraph{Structural: circulation rigidity.}
Under the bridging condition of Definition~\ref{def:Is}, the truth's length-$L$
spectrum is the unique positive circulation of its window-support graph
(Theorem~\ref{cor:same-length-zero}): no other assignment of positive
multiplicities to the observed windows can be balanced and sum to the genome
length. Consequently, on the same-length oriented slice, every spelled
candidate ties the truth under any spectrum-based objective, and no strict
same-length counterexample exists for \emph{any} genome length, read length,
or alphabet. With the complete-spectrum uniqueness step, every such candidate
is in fact a cyclic rotation of the truth (Corollary~\ref{cor:same-length-rotation}).
The extra assumptions this theorem needs --- oriented reads, equal candidate
length, the full-overlap support restriction, and truth candidature --- are
explicit assumptions, stated in Section~\ref{sec:finite};
they do not follow from the published sentence.

\paragraph{Negative: finite-sample reweighting.}
Two separate restrictions are essential to the positive theorem:
truth candidacy (lost in the same-length $AABB\to ABAB$ witness) and
equal length (lost in $AAATT\to AAAATT$). We exhibit finite observations in
which the truth satisfies the bridging conditions and a competitor still has
strictly greater likelihood, under the exact multinomial over all circular
candidates (Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-exact}), under its fixed-length binomial
approximation (Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-binomial}), and under the
reverse-complement molecule reading of the Section~6.2 flow
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-molecule}). The mechanism is empirical read reweighting:
the exact objective scores the \emph{observed} read-type frequencies, and a
competitor can improve its score by reallocating multiplicity toward
over-represented observed types. Allowing a single extra symbol reintroduces
a strict witness (Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-variable-length}). The same failure
survives under the \emph{source's own} read-string predicate of supplement
\S6.4, where coverage and bridging quantify observed read strings matched at
any genomic position: two same-length molecule witnesses
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cex-historical-W1}
and~\ref{thm:cex-historical-W2}) satisfy historical coverage and
matched-string bridging at full strength while beating the truth under both
source-named objectives.

\paragraph{General exact certificates.}
Beyond the special repeat-bridging regime, an internally proved Graver-basis
criterion characterizes rigidity of \emph{any} finite positive integral-flow
support by the absence of feasible elementary lattice perturbations
(Theorem~\ref{thm:graver-rigidity}). On the statistical side, an exact
effective-weight factorization of the fixed-$N$ product-binomial surrogate
gives an if-and-only-if criterion for candidate score dominance over all
nonnegative read-count vectors
(Theorem~\ref{thm:binomial-weights}). These are positive structural
classifications with their own proof content and explicitly restricted
domains.

\paragraph{Population uniqueness.}
We then study maximum likelihood when the read distribution is known exactly,
without sampling fluctuations. In this setting the true genome always
maximizes likelihood --- a Gibbs/KL identity (Lemma~\ref{lem:gibbs})
--- so uniqueness is the only question. Uniqueness is nontrivial: it reduces to
spectrum identifiability, and for primitive genomes satisfying a structural
repeat condition (P2) it is proved in full, with no external premise
(Theorem~\ref{thm:population}). The complete-spectrum uniqueness
step that this theorem needs is the deep combinatorial ingredient; the
maximizer statement itself is routine.

\paragraph{Finite-read error exponent.}
The population theorem identifies when the exact read distribution separates
candidates. A second positive theorem quantifies the finite sample. For a
finite candidate family whose members induce pairwise distinct read
distributions, the probability that any wrong candidate ties or beats the
truth under the exact iid likelihood is at most the sum of the pairwise
Hellinger affinities $\rho(p_S,p_D)=\sum_w\sqrt{p_S(w)p_D(w)}$ raised to the
number of reads (Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-recovery}). Each affinity is strictly
below one under identifiability, so the probability of an exact-ML error
decays exponentially, and any target confidence is reached after finitely many
reads. This bounds the \emph{error} rate, not the likelihood, and it is a
statement about a \emph{finite} candidate class. This premise holds for
the entire primitive-P2 class: Theorem~\ref{thm:p2-finite-class} gives the
intrinsic bound $|D|\le2q^{L-1}$, so
Corollary~\ref{cor:p2-finite-recovery-all} applies without an artificial
length cutoff. The abstract affinity can moreover be made explicit:
Theorem~\ref{thm:p2-uniform-rate} derives from the integer-denominator
structure of the uniform-start read law the uniform gap
$1-\rho(p_S,p_D)\ge1/(8B^4)$ with $B=2q^{L-1}$, hence the kernel-checked bound
\[
  \Pr_S\!\bigl\{\exists D\ \text{nonrotational admissible}:
    L_D(R_1,\dots,R_n)\ge L_S(R_1,\dots,R_n)\bigr\}
  \;\le\; N_C\,e^{-n/(8B^4)},
\]
where $N_C\le\sum_{g=1}^{B}q^{g}$ counts the distinct competitor read laws and
$n\ge8B^4\log(N_C/\delta)$ suffices for error at most $\delta$. The constants
are deliberately loose and exponential in the read length $L$: this is a
positive rate, not a tractability claim. Exact finite-sample success is not even monotone
in sample size (Proposition~\ref{prop:nonmonotone}); the exponential guarantee
is asymptotic, not a monotone curve.

\subsection{How to read this paper}

Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the sequencing and bridging model and the
objectives. Section~\ref{sec:open} states the mathematical question precisely.
Section~\ref{sec:finite} reports the finite-sample results: the positive
rigidity theorem, the finite counterexamples, and the source-predicate
witnesses. Section~\ref{sec:population} introduces the population model, its
uniqueness theorem, and the finite-read exponential error bound together with
the non-monotonicity caveat. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws the failure
modes together and lists what remains open.

Appendix~\ref{app:modeling} explains the modeling choices;
Appendix~\ref{app:ledger} distinguishes source facts, proofs and computations
and carries the proof-to-claim support matrix of Table~\ref{tab:ledger}, which
labels every headline as kernel-checked, paper-level, textbook, external 2016
premise, or open;
Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction} points to the repository verification guide;
Appendix~\ref{app:referents} records the source-referent audit. A reader who wants only
the mathematical story can read Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:discussion}
and treat the appendices as reference.
